# A Universal Union: Social Structures

## 1. Purpose and Scope

A Universal Union does not invent social structure. It receives social structure from the real world, where people already belong to formal institutions such as municipalities and looser informal structures such as households. These arrangements already organize care, duty, authority, conflict, representation, dependency, and shared consequence before any platform attempts to model them.

The purpose of this document is to define how AUU can represent real-world social and civil structures without reducing them to generic groups, chat spaces, voting pools, or administrative permission sets.

For this document:

**Collective** refers to the platform space through which a real-world structure acts inside AUU. A collective may represent a household, neighborhood association, union, school board, religious community, workplace body, public office, municipal structure, federation, movement, or other shared body of concern.

**Container** refers to the configurable structure underneath a collective. It is a design abstraction, not a separate social category and not necessarily a user-facing term. A container describes how a collective is configured: its membership rules, authority limits, roles, privacy boundaries, records, public claims, challenge paths, appeal paths, federation links, and follow-through expectations.

**Profile** refers to a candidate configuration pattern. A profile can help a collective begin from sensible assumptions without treating those assumptions as fixed institutional categories or final product commitments.

The design order is simple: real-world structures come first, collectives are the platform spaces through which those structures act, container configuration describes how those collectives behave, and feature modules provide specific capabilities.

This order matters because AUU is giving real-world structures a way to act through the platform while keeping those actions tied to their source, scope, affected people, and accountability paths.

This document is a best-effort starting framework, not a final institutional theory or technical implementation specification. It does not prescribe the correct roles, offices, processes, appeal rules, or governance forms for every structure AUU may serve. Its purpose is narrower: to identify the configuration domains AUU must preserve so real-world structures can act through the platform without being flattened into generic groups, falsely validated by platform presence, or hidden behind vague claims of collective identity.

This document should be read alongside AUU’s ethics, privacy, and records discussions. Nothing here should be understood as permission for secret civic coordination, hidden power, or unreviewable authority. The basic rule remains: protect persons where exposure creates risk, and preserve reviewability where power is exercised. Because this document often favors brevity over full precision, that rule should guide interpretation wherever privacy, records, or protected participation are mentioned.

The container model is a minimal framework to build from. It should become more accurate through contact with real civic practice and through correction by people with direct experience of the structures AUU aims to represent.

## 2. Collectives and Container Configuration

A real-world structure is any durable or semi-durable human arrangement that organizes identity, belonging, duty, authority, coordination, resources, conflict, or public consequence. Real-world structures may include families, households, neighborhoods, religious communities, unions, schools, workplaces, councils, committees, institutions, municipalities, public offices, courts, federations, movements, informal associations, and temporary coalitions.

AUU should support this range without requiring every structure to formalize itself in the same way. Some structures are loose, temporary, or intentionally informal. Others are legally incorporated, publicly mandated, institutionally durable, or bound by external law. The platform should support this range while making each structure’s authority claims, privacy needs, and accountability paths legible.

The user-facing platform object is the collective. The container is the configuration model that determines how a collective behaves.

A collective gives participants a place to gather, deliberate, govern, represent, publish, challenge, record, federate, and act. The container configuration defines which of these actions are available, who may perform them, under what authority, with what records, under what privacy rules, and with what paths for challenge or review.

A container configuration does not represent an institution in full. It represents the parts of an AUU collective that must be configurable if that collective is going to carry authority, protect privacy, preserve records, receive challenge, federate, or act in the world.

The container model exists between two errors: flattening every structure into the same generic group, and hard-coding one institutional template into the platform. Collectives need enough configurable surface to preserve real differences in authority, privacy, membership, records, public claims, and accountability, without pretending AUU already knows the correct model for every community or institution.

This lets AUU support real-world variety without taking a false position of mastery over it.

## 3. Profiles as Composable Defaults

Container configurations should be composable. A collective may begin with a structural profile that reflects the kind of real-world structure being represented, then activate functional profiles for particular civic actions such as consultation, appeal, ratification, delegated voting, grievance review, or implementation tracking.

Profiles are candidate configuration patterns, not fixed institutional categories or product commitments. They provide starting assumptions, warnings, and accountability concerns that may help later design work. They should not be read as a complete list of platform features or as final templates for real-world institutions.

For example, a municipality may require jurisdictional configuration alongside public consultation, council voting, budget review, implementation tracking, and federation links. A union may require self-governing collective configuration alongside protected membership, grievance review, strike authorization, delegated voting bloc, and federation relationships. A household or care network may require household configuration alongside care-role coordination, emergency access, protected personal records, and limited review paths.

Possible structural profile patterns include:

| Informal association        | Loose groups, movements, issue publics, temporary coalitions, mutual-aid efforts, online communities.             | Lightweight participation, loose membership, limited formal authority.                                            | Informal participation may be inflated into broad representation.                    |
| Household or care structure | Households, family coordination, care networks, emergency support circles.                                        | Strong privacy, limited public surface, care roles, and protected personal records with appropriate review paths. | Privacy may conceal coercion or leave vulnerable people without review.              |
| Self-governing collective   | Tenant associations, unions, clubs, cooperatives, religious communities, project communities.                     | Membership, internal governance, roles, votes, public claims.                                                     | Collective decisions may be presented as more representative than they are.          |
| Institutional body          | Schools, nonprofits, companies, churches, public agencies, professional bodies.                                   | Offices, departments, administrative authority, public-facing obligations.                                        | Institutional power may hide behind private organizational boundaries.               |
| Civil structure             | Councils, committees, hearings, review boards, commissions, departments, ombuds, audit bodies.                    | Defined authority, procedures, records, review paths, parent relationships.                                       | Advisory or procedural bodies may become hidden decision authorities.                |

Profiles may shape a collective as a whole, a substructure such as a council or department, or a defined process. Roles and offices should usually be handled through role configuration rather than whole functional profiles, unless they contain enough internal procedure, records, discretion, or authority to behave as structured civic actors in their own right.

A federation can be treated as a collective with extended configuration for member collectives, representative authority, shared standards, dissent records, exit rules, and inter-collective claims.

When profiles conflict, AUU should resolve priority through explicit configuration while preserving the underlying safeguards: protection for persons, reviewability where power is exercised, limits on authority, and records proportionate to consequence.

The container model remains a configurable abstraction. Profiles adapt it to common structures and functions, but they should evolve as AUU learns from real civic practice.

## 4. Configurable Civic Domains

A container configuration is composed from civic domains.

Profiles provide defaults, but domains define what is actually configurable. A structural profile may suggest that a collective needs protected membership, public records, role definitions, or federation links. A functional profile may suggest that a process needs appeal standing, authority grants, implementation evidence, or dissent records. The domains are the underlying rule areas those profiles configure.

Some domains can be defined with reasonable confidence now. Some can be framed at a general level but require later institutional detail. Some should remain mostly listed until people with direct knowledge of the relevant structures expand them.

The purpose is not to burden every collective with bureaucracy. The purpose is to prevent authority-bearing structures from becoming illegible.

| Domain                          | What it configures                                                                                                                                                          | How far this document goes                      |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Identity boundary               | What real-world structure the collective represents.                                                                                                                        | Define a baseline.                              |
| Default profile                 | Which starting configuration best fits the collective.                                                                                                                      | Define as defaults, not fixed categories.       |
| Membership                      | Who belongs, how belonging is established, and what belonging does or does not authorize.                                                                                   | Define a baseline.                              |
| Representational consent        | When the collective may claim to represent a member’s will, interests, identity, affected status, or public position.                                                       | Define a baseline.                              |
| Authority claims                | What the collective claims power to say, decide, represent, bind, administer, or coordinate.                                                                                | Define a baseline.                              |
| Public surface                  | What the collective exposes externally.                                                                                                                                     | Frame now, expand later.                        |
| Privacy boundaries              | What identities, rosters, ballots, credentials, records, or proceedings require protection.                                                                                 | Define a baseline, expand carefully.            |
| Records                         | What decisions, claims, votes, challenges, role actions, amendments, receipts, or implementation updates must be preserved.                                                 | Define a baseline, expand later.                |
| Roles and offices               | Who holds defined responsibilities, what authority they carry, and how they are reviewed or replaced.                                                                       | Frame now, avoid universal templates.           |
| Authority grants                | What authority is granted, by whom, for what purpose, within what limits, and with what expiration or review.                                                               | Frame now, avoid context-specific overclaiming. |
| Standing rules                  | Who may deliberate, decide, observe, challenge, appeal, provide expertise, or claim affected-party status.                                                                  | Frame now, expand later.                        |
| Processes                       | How proposals, decisions, disputes, amendments, reviews, and records are produced.                                                                                          | Frame now, leave details open.                  |
| Challenge paths                 | How claims, conduct, authority, or decisions can be contested.                                                                                                              | Define a baseline, expand later.                |
| Appeal paths                    | How decisions can be reviewed after they have been made.                                                                                                                    | Frame now, leave details open.                  |
| Federation links                | How the collective relates to broader, parallel, subordinate, overlapping, or coordinating structures.                                                                      | Frame now, expand later.                        |
| Exit and succession             | How members, roles, substructures, or whole collectives leave, fork, dissolve, merge, or change.                                                                            | Frame now, expand later.                        |
| Follow-through expectations     | What evidence, updates, records, or affected-party feedback are expected when authorized action leaves the platform.                                                        | Frame now, expand later.                        |
| Institution-specific procedures | Labor grievances, municipal procurement, school discipline, legal hearings, professional regulation, religious misconduct processes, emergency powers, and similar domains. | List only until better expertise is available.  |

This table is not meant to be complete. It is a first map of the configuration domains AUU collectives are likely to require.

The distinction between defining, framing, and reserving matters. AUU can know that some domains must remain configurable without knowing the correct model for every institution or context. It can also identify areas that may require platform support without yet knowing how that support should be implemented. Some domains may become simple fields, structured rules, specialized modules, cryptographic proofs, interface patterns, or procedures outside the platform. This document should preserve the need for those domains without pretending their final civic or technical form is already understood.

## 5. Baseline Safeguards

Several configuration principles are central enough to restate here. They are not new ethical commitments unique to this document. They translate AUU’s existing ethics, privacy, records, delegation, and accountability principles into safeguards for collectives and container configuration.

**Membership is not decision authority.** A participant may belong to a collective without being eligible to vote in every process, hold every office, access every record, or authorize every public claim.

**Membership is not representational consent.** A participant may belong to a collective without authorizing that collective to represent their will, interests, identity, affected status, or public position in every context. Representation should be scoped. A member may authorize representation for one issue, process, role, statement, or public claim while withholding it in another.

**Authority grants should remain representable.** When a collective grants authority, AUU should be able to represent what authority was granted, who or what granted it, whom it affects, what limits apply, when it expires or requires review, and how it can be challenged or revoked. This does not determine which grants of authority are legitimate in every context. It prevents authority claims from becoming vague, unlimited, or unverifiable.

**Authority requires scope.** No authority-bearing role, body, office, committee, council, collective, or federation should operate as if its authority is unlimited. Scope should include what the authority applies to, whom it affects, what it cannot do, when it expires or requires review, and how it can be challenged.

**Roles are not permission flags.** When a collective defines an authority-bearing role, AUU should be able to represent the role’s source of authority, scope, duties, permissions, limits, required records, conflicts requiring disclosure, review path, and revocation or replacement mechanism. This does not mean AUU defines the correct roles for every collective. It means AUU should not reduce authority-bearing roles to simple permissions.

**Processes should remain legible.** When a collective defines an authority-bearing process, AUU should be able to represent the process well enough for participants to understand how outcomes are produced, who may participate, what records are required, how decisions are reviewed, and how failures can be challenged. This does not mean AUU defines the correct process for every institution. It means AUU should preserve enough structure for process-based authority to remain legible.

**Standing is not membership.** A non-member may lack decision authority while still having challenge standing, appeal standing, affected-party standing, expert standing, observer standing, or witness standing. A collective should be able to protect its membership boundary without excluding every legitimate external challenge.

**Privacy protects persons, not hidden power.** Protected identity, sealed rosters, secret ballots, private credentials, pseudonymous participation, dissident safety, internal care records, and child protection may all be necessary. But privacy should not allow authority to become unverifiable. Where persons require protection, AUU should preserve privacy. Where power is exercised, AUU should preserve reviewability.

**Records preserve civic memory, not total surveillance.** Records may include proposals, decisions, votes, receipts, amendments, minority reports, public challenges, conflict disclosures, role actions, appeal outcomes, federation agreements, implementation updates, and audit trails. Records should distinguish public information, member-visible information, protected information, sealed review material, participant receipts, and historical archive material.

**Oversight applies to watchers.** Moderators, reviewers, witnesses, delegates, maintainers, administrators, summarizers, auditors, ranking systems, and other oversight roles require their own oversight. No structure should only watch others while remaining structurally unwatchable itself.

**Participants should be able to verify whether their authority was used.** Where a collective claims to represent, count, or act on behalf of members, participants should be able to verify whether their own membership, authority grant, delegation, standing, vote, or representational consent was invoked in that process. This verification should not require exposing the participant’s identity, ballot choice, private membership, or unrelated participation history.

**Coordination is not binding vote discipline.** Participants may coordinate around a shared position, form a voting bloc, endorse a proposal, or recommend a common vote. This does not by itself give the bloc authority over each participant’s vote. A stronger form exists when participants explicitly authorize a shared vote or voting authority through a bloc process. That should be treated as delegated or bound voting authority, with clear scope, consent, revocation rules, records, and protection against coercion.

## 6. Public Claims, Consultation, Challenge, and Legitimacy

A public claim is any claim made by a person, role, body, collective, institution, or federation that invokes authority, representation, legitimacy, or collective standing.

AUU should distinguish ordinary expression from authority-bearing public claims.

| Claim type              | Example                                     | Required support                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Personal claim          | “I believe this policy is harmful.”         | Ordinary speech protections.                                      |
| Collective expression   | “Our group opposes this proposal.”          | Clear collective identity and statement authority.                |
| Member decision claim   | “Our members voted to support this.”        | Eligibility rule, voting method, tally, record.                   |
| Representation claim    | “We speak for this neighborhood.”           | Standing basis, authority grant, challenge path.                  |
| Binding authority claim | “This decision binds members.”              | Membership rule, consent boundary, decision process, appeal path. |
| Federation claim        | “Our federation has adopted this position.” | Member consent, federation process, dissent record.               |
| Affected-party claim    | “This group is harmed by the policy.”       | Standing basis, evidence or testimony path.                       |
| Institutional claim     | “This office has approved the action.”      | Office authority, process record, review path.                    |

A collective should not be able to launder authority through vague language such as “the community believes,” “members support,” “the people demand,” or “affected groups agree” without making the basis of the claim legible.

Where a public claim invokes authority, representation, legitimacy, or affected-party standing, AUU should be able to preserve enough context for the claim to be understood and challenged. This may include the claimant, the claimed authority, the process used, the eligible participants, the participation boundary, the representational consent basis, dissent or minority records, unresolved challenges, record location, expiry or review date, and available review path.

This does not mean every public statement requires formal process. A person may speak personally. A loose association may express a shared view. A collective may publish ordinary communications. The stronger requirement applies when a claim asks others to treat the statement as authorized, representative, binding, jurisdictional, institutional, or legitimacy-bearing.

Creating a collective inside AUU should not imply legitimacy by itself. It means a structure, association, claim of association, or shared body of concern has been represented inside the platform. It may correspond to an existing off-platform institution or community, but it may also be newly formed, informal, temporary, online-only, or platform-native. Creation does not automatically mean the collective is truthful, fair, authorized, representative, democratic, legally valid, or morally legitimate. Legitimacy must come from process, standing, records, recognition, law, consent, challenge response, or other context-specific grounds.

A collective is therefore a platform representation, not a legitimacy stamp.

## 7. Real-World Follow-Through

A collective may use AUU to deliberate, authorize, record, or challenge decisions that are later carried out beyond the platform.

This transition matters because many civic decisions are not complete when a vote closes, a council approves a motion, or a collective publishes a statement. A collective may authorize action, but the action still has to be carried out by people, offices, delegates, staff, contractors, volunteers, public servants, institutions, or external authorities.

AUU should therefore distinguish platform authorization from real-world follow-through.

**Platform authorization** means a collective approved, rejected, amended, delegated, or challenged an action according to its defined process.

**Real-world follow-through** means the authorized action was carried out, attempted, delayed, altered, ignored, contested, or completed beyond the platform.

AUU can help preserve the relationship between authorization and follow-through, but it cannot replace civic responsibility. The onus remains on participants, delegates, representatives, affected people, watchdogs, and responsible collectives to ensure that actions taken beyond the platform remain faithful to the decisions, authority grants, limits, and records produced inside the platform.

This is not a defect in the model. Civic systems cannot be made responsible by software alone. AUU may reduce blind trust by preserving records, receipts, challenge paths, evidence, and public claims, but it still depends on civic duty: people must notice, report, verify, challenge, correct, and refuse false claims when platform decisions are misrepresented in the world.

A participant should be able to understand how an authorized decision left the platform and what evidence exists about what happened afterward. This does not mean AUU can perfectly verify every real-world action. Evidence may live inside AUU, outside AUU, or across both. External verification may depend on public records, whistleblowing, affected-party testimony, or procedures AUU does not control.

In those cases, AUU can only make best efforts to preserve truthful reporting. It may record claims, attach evidence, identify uncertainty, preserve dissent, expose conflicts of interest, enable challenge, and distinguish verified facts from unresolved assertions. The important requirement is not that AUU verify every real-world action by itself. The requirement is that authorized action should not become impossible to follow, contest, correct, or review once it leaves the platform.

|Authorized action|Reasonable follow-through evidence|
|---|---|
|Municipality approves road repair|Work order, budget line, contractor record, completion report, public notice, resident confirmation.|
|Union authorizes grievance|Filed grievance record, steward update, employer response, hearing date, outcome record.|
|School board adopts policy|Published policy text, implementation memo, meeting record, appeal path, affected-party feedback.|
|Mutual-aid group approves supply distribution|Inventory record, volunteer assignment, delivery confirmation, recipient or witness feedback where safe.|
|Church or cultural body begins misconduct review|Review body authority, protected process record, response deadlines, outcome summary, appeal path.|
|Household assigns care responsibility|Private acknowledgement, completion note, or limited family review where appropriate.|

The examples above are not universal evidence requirements. They show that follow-through evidence should be proportionate to the action, the risk, the people affected, and the reasonable expectations of the context. The purpose is not to turn every action into surveillance. The purpose is to prevent civic decisions from disappearing after authorization.

Where a collective authorizes real-world action, its configuration should be able to identify the responsible actor, the scope of authority, the expected evidence or update, the challenge path, and any review or expiry point.

AUU should also help participants recognize common forms of implementation failure.

|Failure mode|Description|
|---|---|
|Performative implementation|A structure claims action was taken, but only symbolic or superficial steps occurred.|
|Scope substitution|The approved action is replaced with a narrower, weaker, or different action without renewed authorization.|
|Authority drift|A delegate, office, committee, or contractor uses limited authority to claim broader authority.|
|Hidden modification|Key terms are changed during execution without clear notice to the authorizing body.|
|Selective implementation|Only politically convenient parts of the decision are carried out.|
|False completion|The action is marked complete despite unresolved work, missing evidence, or affected-party dispute.|
|Coerced confirmation|Participants are pressured to affirm completion, consent, satisfaction, or support.|
|Record laundering|Incomplete or misleading records are used to make the implementation appear legitimate.|
|Implementation capture|The actor responsible for execution redirects the decision toward their own interest or faction.|

These failures should remain contestable. A challenge may claim that an action was not completed, exceeded its authority, changed after approval, ignored affected parties, concealed important information, or produced consequences inconsistent with the authorized purpose.

The basic rule is:

**A decision should not vanish once it leaves the platform.**

If AUU helps authorize real-world action, it should also help preserve enough record for that action to be followed, challenged, corrected, audited, repaired, or reversed.

## 8. Domains Requiring Further Civic Modeling

This document can identify the configurable domains AUU collectives require, but it should not pretend to fully model every institution AUU may serve.

Some domains are too dependent on legal systems, institutional customs, professional obligations, cultural practices, workplace conditions, child protection rules, local governance procedures, or lived community knowledge to be fully specified here.

For example, AUU can state that appeal paths, authority-bearing roles, and follow-through evidence should remain configurable. It cannot safely decide the correct form of those things across all institutions or contexts.

For that reason, later contributors should be able to expand the container model by refining domain-specific patterns, modules, records, review paths, or off-platform procedures where appropriate.

Possible areas for later modeling include:

| Area                                   | Why it requires care                                                                                                                           |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Appeal rules                           | Different institutions vary in grounds, timelines, review bodies, stays, escalation, and remedies.                                             |
| Labor and workplace processes          | Grievances, bargaining units, retaliation risks, strike votes, steward authority, and employer-facing claims require specialized knowledge.    |
| Municipal and public administration    | Budgets, procurement, bylaws, public hearings, departments, permits, and implementation records vary by jurisdiction.                          |
| Schools and child-facing institutions  | Students, parents, staff, trustees, administrators, privacy, discipline, and child protection require heightened care.                         |
| Religious and cultural bodies          | Authority, teaching, misconduct review, care processes, tradition, and membership may not map cleanly onto secular organizational forms.       |
| Legal and quasi-legal processes        | Courts, tribunals, ombuds, evidence, jurisdiction, procedural fairness, and appeal rights require legal expertise.                             |
| Federations and treaty-like structures | Shared authority, member autonomy, dissent, exit, sovereignty, and intergovernmental relationships require careful modeling.                   |
| Household and care structures          | Privacy, dependency, disability, emergency access, coercion risk, and external support must be handled without over-publicizing intimate life. |
| Emergency powers                       | Fast action, temporary authority, post-hoc review, rights floors, and abuse prevention require special constraints.                            |

These areas should not be ignored. They should be marked as domains where AUU needs better civic knowledge before claiming mature design.

The container model should remain open to correction. It should define enough structure to prevent generic-group collapse, but not so much that AUU hard-codes poor assumptions about institutions, communities, or procedures it does not yet understand.

## 9. Failure Modes and Design Requirements

AUU should explicitly design against recurring social-structure failures.

| Failure mode                    | Description                                                                                                                                            | Design response                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flattening                      | Different structures are reduced to generic groups.                                                                                                    | Use configurable container domains and composable profiles.                                                                                  |
| Legitimacy confusion            | Creating a collective is mistaken for validating the structure.                                                                                        | Treat collectives as platform representations, not legitimacy stamps.                                                                        |
| False representation            | A group claims to speak for people who did not authorize it.                                                                                           | Require public-claim metadata, representational consent, and challenge paths.                                                                |
| Sealed-roster laundering        | Protected membership is used to claim legitimacy without adequate review.                                                                              | Protect rosters while verifying process and authority claims.                                                                                |
| Federation laundering           | A federation claims broad support while suppressing member dissent.                                                                                    | Preserve member consent, dissent records, exit rules, and local autonomy.                                                                    |
| Bloc coercion                   | A voting bloc, caucus, faction, or coordinated group pressures participants to vote uniformly without explicit delegated authority or safe revocation. | Distinguish coordination from binding authority, require consent and revocation paths, and protect ballot secrecy where secrecy is required. |
| Charismatic capture             | Loyalty to a person replaces accountability to the structure.                                                                                          | Scope roles, preserve records, enable recall and challenge.                                                                                  |
| Procedural fog                  | Rules become too complex for ordinary participants to understand.                                                                                      | Require readable process summaries and constrained governance schemas.                                                                       |
| Algorithmic authority           | Hidden ranking or scoring systems become de facto governance.                                                                                          | Keep algorithmic outputs reviewable, challengeable, and subordinate to civic process.                                                        |
| Expert capture                  | Specialized knowledge becomes unchallengeable authority.                                                                                               | Preserve expert standing without eliminating public challenge.                                                                               |
| Administrative capture          | Offices or staff control outcomes through procedure.                                                                                                   | Log administrative actions and preserve appeal paths.                                                                                        |
| Direct-democracy simplification | Voting is treated as sufficient for all legitimacy.                                                                                                    | Preserve deliberation, rights, records, representation, and review.                                                                          |
| Institutional secrecy           | Privacy for persons becomes secrecy for power.                                                                                                         | Distinguish protected identity from reviewable authority.                                                                                    |
| Restorative theater             | Language of repair is used to avoid consequences.                                                                                                      | Require concrete repair actions, records, and affected-party review.                                                                         |
| Punitive capture                | Accountability tools become factional punishment.                                                                                                      | Separate warnings, findings, sanctions, appeals, and repair paths.                                                                           |
| Implementation laundering       | Weak or misleading evidence is used to claim real-world follow-through.                                                                                | Require proportionate implementation records and challenge paths.                                                                            |

These failure modes produce several design requirements.

|Requirement area|Design requirement|
|---|---|
|Representation|AUU must be able to represent real-world social and civil structures through collectives without reducing them to generic groups.|
|Container configuration|AUU must treat container configuration as the model that describes how a collective behaves.|
|Structural distinctions|AUU must preserve distinctions between persons, associations, collectives, institutions, civil structures, jurisdictions, roles, offices, processes, and federations where those distinctions affect authority, privacy, representation, standing, records, or accountability.|
|Profiles|AUU must support default profiles without treating those profiles as fixed institutional categories or final product commitments.|
|Composability|AUU must allow collectives to compose structural and functional profiles where appropriate.|
|Configurable domains|AUU must preserve the ability to configure membership, representational consent, roles, offices, authority grants, standing rules, records, privacy boundaries, public claims, challenge paths, appeal paths, federation links, exit rules, succession rules, and follow-through expectations where those domains are relevant.|
|Membership and consent|AUU must distinguish membership from representational consent.|
|Roles and authority|AUU must be able to represent authority-bearing roles without reducing them to simple permission flags.|
|Standing|AUU must distinguish member authority from public challenge standing.|
|Public claims|AUU must be able to attach reviewable process metadata to public claims where those claims invoke authority, representation, or legitimacy.|
|Consultation and challenge|AUU must distinguish public consultation from public challenge.|
|Privacy and reviewability|AUU must protect rosters, ballots, pseudonymous participation, private credentials, and sensitive records where exposure creates risk, while preserving reviewability where power is exercised.|
|Delegated voting blocs|AUU must be able to represent delegated voting blocs only where authority is explicit, scoped, revocable, and verifiable.|
|Federations|AUU must be able to represent federations without erasing local autonomy, member dissent, exit rights, or lower-level standing.|
|Appeals and review|AUU must preserve the possibility of appeal or review pathways proportionate to consequence, while leaving domain-specific appeal models open to later refinement.|
|Restoration and containment|AUU must preserve space for restorative flows where safety allows and protective containment where safety or system integrity requires immediate action.|
|Anti-laundering|AUU must prevent authority from being laundered through vague group language, sealed membership, delegation chains, federation claims, algorithmic ranking, bloc discipline, or weak implementation records.|
|Oversight|AUU must make oversight representable for authority-bearing roles, including roles responsible for moderation, review, verification, maintenance, or interpretation.|
|Records|AUU must preserve records in a way that supports civic memory without turning participation into total surveillance.|
|Participant verification|AUU must allow participants to verify whether their membership, authority grant, delegation, standing, vote, or representational consent was used by a collective in an authority-bearing process.|
|Follow-through|AUU must distinguish platform authorization from real-world follow-through, while leaving the final technical and procedural forms of implementation evidence open to later design.|
|Domain expertise|AUU must leave room for domain-specific modeling by people with direct knowledge of the relevant institutions, communities, procedures, and risks.|
