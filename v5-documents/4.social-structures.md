# A Universal Union: Social Structures

**Status:** Conceptual model for representing groups and institutions; not a universal theory of social organization

**Primary readers:** Governance, civic-design, product, privacy, safety, and institutional contributors

## 1. Purpose and central rule

People already organize care, identity, work, belief, authority, resources, conflict, and shared consequences. They do so through households, friendships, informal groups, online communities, workplaces, unions, schools, religious bodies, companies, public offices, municipalities, movements, temporary coalitions, and many other arrangements.

Some structures exist long before AUU. Others may first form through online or platform interaction. The controlling rule is therefore not “offline first.” It is:

> People, relationships, and accepted rules create a social structure. A platform object only represents the structure and the claims made through it.

Creating a collective in AUU does not prove that the people exist as claimed, consent to representation, accept the rules, possess legal authority, speak for anyone else, or deserve legitimacy. Those claims require their own evidence, process, recognition, and challenge paths.

This document explains how AUU can preserve real differences among social structures without flattening them into generic groups or pretending to know the correct institutional form for every community.

## 2. A minimal model

The earlier draft used *structure*, *collective*, *container*, *profile*, and *domain* so often that the model became difficult to hold. The replacement keeps only distinctions that change behavior, authority, privacy, or review.

| Term | Meaning | What its creation proves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social structure | An actual relationship or arrangement among people that organizes belonging, duty, authority, resources, care, conflict, or consequence. It may be formal or informal, old or new, offline, online, or mixed. | That the relationship exists only to the extent supported by real participation, rules, law, records, or other evidence. |
| Collective | The AUU space through which a claimed social structure acts. | Only that someone created a space and made a claim about its purpose or identity. |
| Collective configuration | The rules governing membership, roles, authority, privacy, decisions, records, challenge, appeal, exit, federation, and follow-through. | That these are the rules published in AUU—not that they are fair, accepted, lawful, or followed. |
| Configuration profile | An editable starting template for a common kind of structure or process. | Nothing about the actual collective until the profile is adopted and used. |
| Role or office | A defined responsibility or authority within or outside the collective. | Only the title unless its source, scope, permissions, duties, and review are connected. |
| Federation | A structure through which collectives coordinate under shared rules while retaining some autonomy. | Only the federation relationship and authority that participating collectives actually authorized and the receiving context accepts. |

*Container* may remain an internal software or schema term for the configurable object beneath a collective. Reader-facing documents should normally say *collective configuration*.

## 3. What a collective needs to declare

Not every collective needs heavy governance. A book club, family care group, city government, online movement, and labor federation should not face the same configuration burden. Requirements should scale with power, risk, and the strength of the claims being made.

When relevant, the configuration must be able to express:

| Area | What must be understandable |
| --- | --- |
| Identity and purpose | What relationship, institution, office, place, or shared concern the collective claims to represent. |
| Membership | Who belongs, who decides that, what proves membership, and what membership does not authorize. |
| Representation | When the collective may speak or act for members, affected people, an institution, a jurisdiction, or another body. |
| Authority | What may be decided or done, by whom, under which source, within which limit, and until when. |
| Roles and offices | Duties, permissions, conflicts, required records, appointment, review, replacement, and removal. |
| Processes | How proposals, decisions, amendments, grievances, challenges, appeals, and emergencies work. |
| Privacy | Which identities, rosters, ballots, proceedings, evidence, and records are public, participant- or member-visible, protected review, or private. |
| Public claims | Who may issue them and which process record must support them. |
| Records | What is preserved, who may inspect it, how long it remains, and which safety or legal exceptions apply. |
| Challenge and appeal | Who may contest which action, what response is required, who reviews it, and how the process reaches closure. |
| External relationships | Parent or child structures, overlapping jurisdictions, contracts, laws, credentials, and federation links. |
| Exit and continuity | Leaving, expulsion, forks, mergers, dissolution, succession, and disputed claims to the collective's identity or history. |
| Follow-through | Who carries authorized action into the world, what evidence or update is expected, and how deviation is challenged. |

The system should generate a plain-language process summary from the configuration. A technically valid rule that participants cannot understand is not enough.

## 4. Profiles are defaults, not social truth

Profiles can lower the cost of configuration and surface common risks. They should not hard-code one culture's institutional categories.

Possible starting profiles include:

| Profile | Useful for | Default emphasis | Main warning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Informal association | Temporary coalitions, movements, mutual aid, project groups, and online communities. | Light membership, personal speech, advisory decisions, simple records. | Informal participation may be inflated into representation. |
| Private care network | Households, family coordination, disability support, and emergency care circles. | Strong privacy, limited public surface, consent, dependency safeguards, and protected review. | Privacy may conceal coercion or leave a dependent person without outside recourse. |
| Self-governing collective | Cooperatives, tenant groups, unions, clubs, congregations, and member associations. | Membership, internal rules, roles, votes, public claims, challenge, and exit. | Leadership or a narrow turnout may be presented as the will of all members. |
| Institution or public body | Companies, schools, nonprofits, public agencies, councils, and offices. | External authority sources, departments, offices, conflicts, public duties, records, and appeal. | Institutional power may hide behind private administration or technical procedure. |
| Federation | Alliances, councils of collectives, unions of locals, and intermunicipal or transnational bodies. | Member authority, shared rules, dissent, representation, recognition, exit, and inter-collective records. | The larger body may absorb local autonomy or launder unsupported authority. |

A collective can combine profiles or ignore them. A municipality may add consultation, budget review, implementation tracking, and appeal processes. A union may add protected membership, grievance review, strike authorization, and federation ratification. A profile should never decide a rights, authority, or privacy question silently.

When defaults conflict, non-overridable safeguards must be named. “Explicit configuration” is not a sufficient answer if an administrator can simply turn off affected-person standing, safety review, ballot secrecy, or another protection the process claims to guarantee.

## 5. Membership, consent, standing, and authority

These relationships must remain separate.

**Membership is not decision authority.** A member may be unable to vote in a particular process, hold an office, inspect protected evidence, or administer the system.

**Membership is not representational consent.** A person may join without authorizing every statement, federation action, voting bloc, or public claim.

**Standing is not membership.** A non-member may have standing to observe, submit evidence, challenge, appeal, act as an expert or witness, or claim direct effect without receiving a member vote.

**An office is not platform authority.** A legal or social title must be connected to the permissions and records it requires. AUU may record an office created by law, charter, contract, election, appointment, tradition, or another source. The platform does not re-create that source by displaying the title.

**Authority is always scoped.** A grant should identify its source, holder, action, affected people, place or issue, limits, duration, review, and revocation or replacement rule.

**Representation is a claim that needs a basis.** The basis may be a member decision, delegation, law, charter, office, guardianship, contract, ratification, or another accepted source. “This group is affected” may create standing to be heard. It does not automatically create permission to speak with the group's authority.

### 5.1 External authority and member consent can disagree

A union officer, guardian, elected official, director, trustee, judge, or public administrator may possess authority under law or charter that was not granted through an AUU vote. AUU should record that external source and its claimed scope rather than pretending all authority begins on the platform.

Members may still dispute the office, challenge its conduct, preserve dissent, or pursue recall, appeal, election, court review, or another remedy. The platform cannot erase external authority merely because internal opinion opposes it. It also must not treat external legality as proof of moral legitimacy or member consent.

The record should show both claims and the processes capable of changing each one.

### 5.2 Participants should be able to inspect use of their authority

Where a collective claims to count, bind, or represent a participant, the participant should be able to inspect whether their membership, consent, delegation, or vote was used under the stated rule.

This requirement inherits the coercion problem described in the feature document. The system must not promise a transferable proof of a secret vote or protected association. It may need a participant-facing verification method that supports correction without allowing an employer, buyer, family member, faction, or state to enforce compliance. That construction remains research.

## 6. Public claims and named recognition

Anyone may speak personally. Stronger language asks others to accept a stronger authority claim.

| Claim | Questions the record must answer |
| --- | --- |
| “Our group supports this.” | Which group and who could authorize the statement? |
| “Our members voted for this.” | Who counted as a member, which rule applied, what was the turnout and result, and what privacy limits protect the record? |
| “We speak for these residents.” | Which residents, under what mandate or standing, for which purpose, until when, and with which challenge path? |
| “This decision binds members.” | What source allows binding authority, which due process applies, and how can the decision be appealed or changed? |
| “This office approved the action.” | What creates the office, who operated it, what was its scope, and which review applies? |
| “Our federation adopted this position.” | Which collectives participated, what authority did each grant, how was dissent preserved, and who is expected to recognize the result? |

When a public claim uses phrases such as “the community agrees,” “members demand,” or “affected people support,” the system should require it to identify the participating body, turnout, dissent, and mandate.

AUU may label observable process facts: public roster, sealed roster, secret ballot, unresolved challenge, protected review, expired mandate, or disputed office. It should not apply a universal “legitimate” badge.

Recognition always belongs to someone. Members may recognize a representative. A court may recognize an office. A federation may recognize a collective. A receiving institution may accept a credential or decision. Different recognizers may disagree.

Technical verification can show that a stated process occurred. It cannot force another actor to accept the result as morally, legally, or politically authoritative.

## 7. Privacy, private coordination, and reviewability

The earlier draft prohibited “secret civic coordination” too broadly. Legitimate civic life often requires privacy: secret ballots, labor organizing, dissident planning, whistleblowing, protected legal strategy, abuse-related safety planning, intimate care, and internal preparation.

The ethical target is not secrecy itself. It is the hidden exercise of consequential authority.

### 7.1 Visibility follows function and risk

| Context | Likely default | Required caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informal private preparation | Private, or visible only to defined participants or members. | It cannot by itself support an external mandate. |
| Household or care network | Private with narrowly scoped protected review. | Dependence, child safety, disability, abuse, and unequal exit may require trusted outside recourse. |
| Member association | Participant- or member-visible deliberation with public or protected records for authority-bearing claims. | Employers, landlords, hostile states, or opponents must not be able to map members casually. |
| Public institution | Public process and records where law and safety permit, with personal information protected. | Administrative secrecy, legal restrictions, and vulnerable participants need specific treatment. |
| Dissident or high-risk collective | Sealed roster, strong context separation, protected review, and limited public metadata. | Insider knowledge, networks, devices, and writing may still expose the group; no anonymity guarantee is justified. |

Public, participant- or member-visible, protected review, and private are distinct access modes. *Auditable* is incomplete unless the collective names the reviewer, evidence, safeguards, public output, consequence, and appeal.

### 7.2 Roster protection

Public rosters suit some open associations and offices. Restricted or sealed rosters may be essential for workers, tenants, dissidents, refugees, stigmatized minorities, survivors, or other vulnerable groups.

A sealed roster must not become an unreviewable source of voting weight or representation. The collective may need privacy-preserving counts, protected independent review, bounded credentials, or another method. Each method must state what it proves and what it leaves uncertain.

### 7.3 Oversight must be representable

Moderators, auditors, reviewers, administrators, eligibility issuers, ombuds, maintainers, and summarizers exercise power. Their authority, conflicts, actions, records, review, and replacement must be representable too. A collective cannot claim accountability if the people and systems doing the accounting are structurally unaccountable.

## 8. Conflicting structures and claims

Real institutions do not stay neatly aligned with platform records. Organizations split. Offices change hands. Laws conflict with member preferences. Several groups claim the same constituency. Jurisdictions overlap. A usable model must preserve the conflict instead of choosing an “official” actor through an unexplained platform default.

| Conflict | Required system behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Two collectives claim one institution | Keep the claims distinct; show their asserted identity, evidence, recognizers, challenges, and status. Apply an official label only relative to a named law, institution, federation, or other recognizer. |
| A collective splits | Preserve the pre-split record, disputed cause, member or office transitions, obligations, names, assets where relevant, and each branch's continuity claim. |
| A legal office conflicts with member consent | Record the external authority and the internal dissent separately. Link the challenge, recall, election, court, charter, or other process capable of changing each claim. |
| Jurisdictions or mandates overlap | Show the scope of each authority, the people and effects in conflict, the rule for priority or negotiation, and unresolved dispute. |
| One person appears through several memberships or delegation paths | Preserve legitimate multiple roles while preventing the same underlying authority from being counted more than the process allows. Resolve conflicts before tallying. |
| The off-platform institution changes | Record the source and date of the change; update future authority without rewriting earlier records. |
| A role holder or institutional operator is replaced | Transfer only the authority granted to the role, require fresh control authorization, and retain institutional history. |
| A platform-native group claims an external constituency | Allow the association to act for itself while requiring separate evidence before accepting representation of people beyond those who actually authorized it. |
| A collective claims to protect an absent or dependent person | Preserve the person's affected standing and safety while requiring a bounded guardianship, care, advocacy, or review basis. Do not treat inability to object as consent. |

Conflict status should be visible wherever the related authority is used. A receiving process may decide which claimant it accepts under its own rule. AUU's role is to preserve the provenance and disagreement needed to make that decision reviewable.

## 9. From platform authorization to real-world follow-through

A vote or authorization is not the same as action. People, offices, staff, contractors, volunteers, public servants, institutions, and external authorities still have to carry the decision into the world.

AUU should connect an authorization to a follow-through record containing, where relevant:

- the responsible actor and authority source;
- the action and limits;
- expected milestones or evidence;
- deadlines and review dates;
- changes that require renewed authorization;
- reports from affected people;
- conflicts of interest;
- challenge and appeal paths; and
- the current status.

Useful statuses include authorized, planned, in progress, claimed complete, independently reviewed, disputed, blocked, altered, abandoned, and superseded. A status should name who asserted or reviewed it.

Evidence varies by context:

| Authorized action | Possible evidence, subject to context and privacy |
| --- | --- |
| Municipality approves road repair | Work order, budget entry, contractor record, completion report, public notice, and resident observation. |
| Union authorizes a grievance | Filing record, steward update, employer response, hearing date, settlement, or outcome. |
| School board changes a policy | Adopted text, implementation guidance, training record, appeal path, and affected-person feedback. |
| Mutual-aid group distributes supplies | Inventory, volunteer assignment, delivery confirmation, and protected recipient or witness feedback. |
| Religious or cultural body opens a misconduct review | Review body's authority, protected case record, deadlines, permitted outcome summary, and appeal. |
| Household assigns a care duty | Private acknowledgement, completion note, or narrowly authorized review. |

AUU cannot determine the truth of every external event. Public records may be incomplete. Whistleblowers may conflict with officials. A completion report may be false. A witness may be mistaken. The platform should preserve sources, provenance, uncertainty, corrections, and competing claims rather than convert a submitted document into a “verified fact” automatically.

Common implementation failures include:

- symbolic activity presented as completion;
- a weaker or different action substituted without renewed authority;
- a delegate or office exceeding its scope;
- key terms changed during execution;
- only convenient parts implemented;
- pressured participants confirming success;
- incomplete evidence used to launder the record; and
- the implementing actor redirecting the decision toward its own interest.

Software can make these patterns easier to follow. It cannot replace civic responsibility. Participants, delegates, representatives, watchdogs, affected people, and responsible institutions must still notice, report, verify, challenge, and correct action outside AUU.

The basic rule is: **a decision should not disappear when it leaves the platform.**

## 10. Federation without absorption

A federation is a collective relationship among collectives. It may coordinate standards, resources, campaigns, representation, decisions, services, or shared problems. It should not erase the internal governance or dissent of its members.

A federation configuration needs to state:

- which collectives are members and who recognizes that membership;
- how a collective joins, leaves, is suspended, or is removed;
- what each membership class may do;
- how member authority becomes federation authority;
- whether voting power is equal, membership-based, delegated, weighted, or otherwise derived;
- how protected membership claims are reviewed;
- how local dissent and subgroup positions remain visible;
- what the federation can claim externally;
- how conflicts, appeals, and rights-floor disputes are reviewed;
- what happens when a member splits, merges, or changes its authority; and
- which shared decisions bind which members.

Federation membership is a claim of relationship, not a universal credential. One federation may recognize a collective that another rejects. A legal body may accept a federation decision while another institution treats it as advocacy. The record must identify the expected recognizer rather than imply that authority travels automatically.

Technical standards can help collectives exchange proposals, credentials, records, and proofs. They cannot make the receiving collective accept the issuer, mandate, delegation, or governance rule. Interoperability preserves the possibility of communication and migration. It does not settle political recognition.

Exit must remain meaningful, but it cannot be the only protection. A member may be unable to leave without losing livelihood, care, safety, home, community, legal status, or access to shared resources. Effects may also cross the federation boundary. Review, rights floors, and affected-person standing remain necessary even when a formal exit rule exists.

## 11. Areas that require domain knowledge

AUU can define common relationships without pretending that one process fits every institution. The following areas require people with direct knowledge and, in some cases, legal or professional review:

| Area | Questions that cannot be answered generically |
| --- | --- |
| Labor and workplaces | Bargaining units, retaliation, grievances, strike authority, steward roles, employer access, and labor law. |
| Municipal and public administration | Jurisdiction, procurement, budgets, bylaws, hearings, offices, public records, and implementation duties. |
| Schools and child-facing institutions | Student, parent, staff, trustee, privacy, discipline, capacity, consent, and child-protection relationships. |
| Households and care | Dependence, disability, emergency access, guardianship, abuse, privacy, and realistic exit. |
| Religious and cultural bodies | Tradition, teaching authority, membership, care, misconduct review, conscience, and external law. |
| Courts and quasi-legal bodies | Evidence, jurisdiction, procedural fairness, protected records, remedies, and appeal. |
| Federations and treaty-like relationships | Sovereignty, shared authority, dissent, exit, recognition, and overlapping legal orders. |
| Emergency action | Urgency, narrow scope, duration, rights, automatic expiry, after-action review, and abuse prevention. |

Profiles should not be called mature merely because they contain fields for these subjects. Maturity requires evidence that the fields match real practice, protect the people involved, and remain understandable to participants.

## 12. Failure modes and requirements

| Failure | Required response |
| --- | --- |
| Generic-group collapse | Preserve only the structural distinctions that change authority, privacy, standing, records, or review. |
| Platform legitimacy stamp | State that creation records a claim; require named sources and recognizers for stronger authority. |
| False representation | Separate membership, advocacy, standing, office, consent, delegation, and mandate. |
| Sealed-roster laundering | Protect members while stating what review or proof supports counts and representation. |
| Federation laundering | Preserve member authorization, voting basis, dissent, recognition, and exit. |
| Bloc coercion | Require explicit scope and revocation; analyze ballot and delegation proof against coercion. |
| Charismatic or administrative capture | Define role limits, decisions, conflicts, records, removal, and appeal. |
| Procedural fog | Generate readable summaries and test participant comprehension. |
| Algorithmic authority | Treat classifications and scores as contestable interpretations, not governance facts. |
| Institutional secrecy | Protect personal and safety information while preserving appropriate review of consequential authority. |
| Punitive capture | Separate allegation, warning, finding, sanction, appeal, and repair. |
| Implementation laundering | Link authorization to named actors, proportionate evidence, status, affected-person feedback, and challenge. |

AUU cannot **prevent** all authority laundering, capture, coercion, or false reporting. The credible requirement is to make them harder to perform, easier to detect, bounded in effect, and open to challenge and repair.

## 13. Conclusion

The social-structure model can be remembered in four statements:

1. People and relationships create structures; platform objects represent them.
2. Creating a collective does not create legitimacy.
3. Membership, standing, consent, office, delegation, representation, and recognition are different relationships.
4. The stronger a collective's claim of authority, the clearer its source, scope, record, follow-through, and challenge path must become.

Everything more specialized belongs in configuration profiles developed with the people who understand the institution and bear its risks. AUU should make vague authority claims harder to pass as valid without pretending to master every form of human organization in advance.
