# A Universal Union: Controlled Glossary

**Status:** Draft terminology standard for the v5 documents

This glossary controls terms that carry the same meaning across several AUU documents. A document may use a narrower meaning when it says so. Ordinary words used in their ordinary sense do not need to be looked up here.

## A

### Affected person

A person who may experience a material consequence from a proposal, decision, institution, or action. An affected person may have standing to be heard or to challenge even when they are not a member and cannot vote in the process.

### Appeal

A request to review a decision after it has been made. An appeal must identify who may file, the accepted grounds, the deadline, the reviewer, the possible outcomes, and any further review. It is different from a challenge raised during an active process.

### Astroturfing

Organized activity made to look like spontaneous public support or opposition. Astroturfing may use paid participants, coordinated accounts, concealed sponsors, repeated messages, false local identities, or manufactured organizations. Coordination alone is not astroturfing; the deception about its source or independence is central.

### Attestation

A scoped claim made by one actor about another fact or actor—for example, “I witnessed this onboarding” or “this person belongs to this organization.” An attestation proves only what it states under the assurance of its issuer. It is not general trust, legal identity, or moral approval.

### Audit or auditable

A review of whether a process, record, or system followed stated rules. The word is incomplete unless the document identifies who can review, what evidence they can inspect, which access mode applies, how independent the reviewer is, and what can happen after a failure is found. An audit can establish rule compliance; it does not by itself prove that the rules were fair or the underlying claims true.

### Authority

Permission or accepted power to perform a defined action in a defined context. An authority claim should name its source, holder, scope, limits, affected people, duration, revocation or review rule, and the actor or process expected to recognize it.

### Authority-bearing claim or process

A claim or process that asks others to treat an action, decision, statement, role, or representation as authorized. “I support this” is ordinary speech. “Our members authorized this statement” is authority-bearing and requires a reviewable basis.

### Authority laundering

Making an unsupported or limited claim appear more authoritative by passing it through a collective, office, delegation chain, federation, algorithm, credential, sealed roster, or vague phrase such as “the community agrees.”

## B

### Base identity

The protected identity layer used to establish that a participant satisfies the system's bounded personhood or account-uniqueness requirements. It is not automatically the participant's legal identity, public profile, eligibility, pseudonym, or participation history.

### Binding decision

A decision that has authority under a stated rule to change rights, duties, resources, roles, policy, access, or action. A binding decision may still depend on an external institution or law for real-world enforcement.

## C

### Challenge

A structured objection to a claim, process, authority, omission, record, or consequence. A challenge is not automatically a veto. It should have a target, basis, standing label, status, response rule, review or appeal path, safety controls, and record policy.

### Civic actor

A person, collective, institution, office, delegate, or federation acting in a civic process. The term does not imply equal rights, human personhood, or legitimacy across those actor types.

### Civic infrastructure

Shared systems and rules that let people and groups participate in public or collective life. In AUU this includes identity, deliberation, decision-making, delegation, records, challenge, follow-through, repair, and federation. It does not mean one compulsory government or application.

### Civic memory

Records preserved so later participants can understand what was proposed, decided, authorized, contested, attempted, and learned. Civic memory should be proportionate to consequence and must not become a permanent map of every private act.

### Civic standing

See **standing**.

### Collective

The AUU space through which an association, group, institution, office, coalition, or other shared body acts. The people or relationship may exist before AUU or may first form through online or platform interaction. Creating the space records a claimed collective; it does not prove that the collective is representative, lawful, democratic, fair, or legitimate.

### Collective decision

A decision attributed to a collective under its stated process. It is preferable to *shared will* when the record shows a defined decision, because it does not imply that every member or affected person agrees.

### Configuration profile

A reusable starting pattern for configuring a collective or process. A profile may suggest membership, privacy, role, record, decision, and challenge defaults. It is not a fixed social category and should be changed when the real context requires it.

### Consent

Specific, informed, understandable, and revocable agreement where revocation remains possible. Membership, silence, account creation, or participation in one action does not automatically create consent to wider representation, exposure, analysis, or authority.

### Container

An internal design term for the configurable rules beneath a collective. The current drafts prefer *collective configuration* in reader-facing prose because the extra noun rarely helps ordinary readers.

### Coercion resistance

Design that reduces another person's ability to force, buy, or verify a participant's sensitive action. In voting, this may require that a voter cannot reliably prove their ballot choice to an employer, buyer, family member, party, or state. A screenshot-resistant interface is not enough; the requirement depends on the complete voting and threat model.

### Credential

A claim issued by some source and held or presented by a participant—for example, residency, membership, age, or professional qualification. A credential is not automatically public, universally accepted, or evidence of moral worth.

## D

### Delegate

A person, role, or collective given limited authority by another actor. Delegation should identify scope, permitted actions, duration, onward-delegation rules, review, and revocation.

### Deliberation

Structured reasoning before or around a decision. It may include questions, evidence, testimony, definitions, alternatives, objections, amendments, summaries, and unresolved disagreement. It is more than an unstructured comment stream.

### Decision authority

The permission to participate in or determine a formal outcome. It is distinct from the ability to observe, speak, provide expertise, advocate, or challenge.

## E

### Eligibility

Whether an actor satisfies the stated conditions for a particular action or process. Eligibility may depend on membership, residency, age, office, credential, affected status, personhood, or another accepted rule. It should be checked with the least disclosure reasonably possible.

### End-to-end verifiability

A voting property that lets participants and observers gain evidence that ballots were handled and tallied according to the voting design. It does not automatically provide coercion resistance, anonymity, usable security, fair eligibility, or a legitimate surrounding process.

## F

### Federation

A relationship in which collectives coordinate under shared rules while retaining some internal autonomy. Federation may include common standards, decisions, representation, and records. It is not merely data exchange. Technical connection does not force one collective to recognize another's identity, delegation, mandate, or authority.

### Follow-through

What happens after an authorized action leaves AUU. Follow-through may be completed, delayed, changed, blocked, disputed, or abandoned. AUU can connect reports and evidence to the authorization; it cannot make software alone responsible for real-world execution.

## G

### Governance

The rules and processes by which authority is created, used, limited, reviewed, and changed. Governance includes more than voting: agenda setting, membership, roles, deliberation, implementation, records, challenge, appeal, and amendment may all matter.

## I

### Identity bridge

Information or a relationship that allows protected civic activity to be connected to a real person or another context. Issuer records, IP addresses, timing, device data, social graphs, writing style, recovery processes, and insiders can all create bridges. AUU can try to minimize or bound them; it cannot honestly promise that none exist.

### Inclusion verification

Evidence that an action or ballot was included in the relevant record or tally. Inclusion verification must be distinguished from evidence of the content of a secret choice. Whether it can remain useful without enabling coercion is a voting-design question, not a wording problem.

### Institutional account

A marked account through which an office or organization acts. It is not a human participant in the personhood layer. Its credibility comes from its claimed authority, operators, governance, record, external status, and responses to challenge.

### Interpretation

A summary, classification, score, cluster, warning, chart, model output, or other attempt to explain records. Interpretations should state sources and uncertainty, remain challengeable, and never silently replace the underlying record.

## L

### Legitimacy

The degree to which an authority, decision, process, or institution deserves acceptance under relevant ethical, social, procedural, and sometimes legal standards. AUU can preserve evidence relevant to legitimacy; it cannot grant legitimacy merely by hosting a process or applying a label.

## M

### Mandate

A limited authorization to act, decide, or represent. A mandate should identify who granted it, what it permits, whom it concerns, when it ends, and how it can be reviewed or revoked.

### Member

A person or actor accepted within a collective under its membership rule. Membership does not automatically create voting rights, access to every record, consent to every public claim, or authority to represent other members.

### Metadata

Information about an action or record rather than its full content—for example, time, process type, status, issuer, or size. Metadata can still expose sensitive patterns and must not be treated as harmless by default.

### Minority report

A preserved explanation of substantial dissent or an unresolved alternative after a process reaches a dominant outcome. A minority report does not cancel the outcome, but prevents the official record from pretending that the disagreement disappeared.

## O

### Opaque credential

A credential whose sensitive details are not ordinarily readable from the wallet, public profile, or process record. The holder reveals only the proof needed for the current purpose where the design permits it.

## P

### Participant receipt

A participant-facing artifact or view concerning an action. The phrase must always say what the receipt proves. A submission acknowledgement, inclusion check, delegation-status view, and proof of ballot choice create different safety risks. Secret processes should avoid transferable evidence that enables coercion.

### Personhood

In these documents, a bounded technical or procedural claim that an account corresponds to one eligible human participant for a stated purpose. It is not a philosophical proof of human essence, a legal identity record, or a guarantee that one biometric method can prevent every duplicate or impersonation.

### Process proof

Evidence that a process followed specified rules—for example, that eligibility checks ran, quorum was met, a deadline was respected, or a tally matches accepted inputs. It does not prove that the proposal was wise, the source data true, or the rules legitimate.

### Protected review

A visibility mode in which authorized reviewers inspect evidence that cannot safely be public. The process should state who qualifies as a reviewer, what they may inspect, which safeguards bind them, what public metadata or findings may be released, and how their conclusions can be challenged.

### Pseudonym

An operational identity used without automatically exposing the person's legal identity or other contexts. A pseudonym may be public, private, or highly protected. It reduces some exposure but may still be linked through behavior, networks, devices, issuers, insiders, or outside information.

### Public

Available for anyone to inspect. *Public* must not be used as a loose synonym for participant- or member-visible, protected review, or auditable.

### Public claim

A statement directed beyond private preparation. A public claim becomes authority-bearing when it asks others to accept that a group decided, authorized, represents, binds, or legitimately speaks for someone.

## R

### Recognition

Acceptance of a claim, identity, credential, mandate, or authority by a named actor or process. The documents should identify the recognizer—for example, members, a law, a federation, a court, a receiving collective, or an AUU conformity process—rather than use *recognized* as if acceptance were universal.

### Record

Preserved information about what was submitted, decided, authorized, changed, challenged, or done. A record may show that a claim was made; it does not prove the claim true. Record access may be public, participant- or member-visible, protected review, or private.

### Reputation

Contextual history about conduct, contributions, warnings, corrections, obligations, or outcomes. Reputation should remain tied to its source and scope. It is not proof of personhood, universal trust, or a permanent score of human worth.

### Rights floor

A minimum set of protections that a governance process may not override merely through local preference or majority vote. In the current AUU project, the floor is an ethical commitment, not a claim of existing universal legal authority. Any implementation must publish who defines, interprets, amends, enforces, and reviews its floor and what happens when federated communities disagree.

### Roster

The membership list of a collective. A public roster is openly inspectable; a restricted roster has limited access; a sealed roster hides ordinary member inspection and relies on bounded proofs or protected review. A sealed roster protects association but cannot by itself justify a claim about membership size or representation.

## S

### Scope

The boundary of an action, role, authority, record, credential, reputation signal, or decision. Scope may identify topic, place, collective, people affected, permitted action, duration, or another limit.

### Shared will

A phrase the replacement drafts generally avoid because it can imply that a population has one discoverable will. Prefer the exact object shown by the evidence: agreement, disagreement, preference, decision, mandate, turnout, consent, or unresolved conflict.

### Standing

A recognized relationship that allows an actor to perform a particular kind of action in a process. Standing is always scoped. Useful forms include standing to observe, speak, submit evidence, challenge, appeal, vote, represent, act as an expert, act as a witness, or claim direct effect. One form does not automatically grant another.

### Submission acknowledgement

Evidence that a system received an attempted action. It may not prove that the action was accepted, included in the final record, or counted in an outcome.

### Sybil attack

An attack in which one actor creates or controls many apparent participants to gain influence, manufacture support, evade limits, or corrupt trust. Named after a fictional person with multiple identities; the term does not refer to ordinary pseudonym use by one person within permitted rules.

## T

### Threat model

A stated account of what must be protected, from whom, under which capabilities and assumptions. Privacy and security claims are meaningful only against a threat model. Protection from casual public browsing is not protection from a credential issuer, platform operator, employer, device seizure, network observer, or state intelligence service.

### Trust

In AUU's current design, a contextual relationship or signal of human recognition. It is not friendship, agreement, expertise, popularity, or moral approval. The exact trust model remains a research question and must not be reduced to one hidden universal score.

### Trust graph

A network of scoped recognition or attestation relationships used to help interpret whether participation appears grounded in real people and communities. A graph can expose associations and reproduce inequality; its design, limits, consent rules, and use require research.

## V

### Verifiability

The ability to check a defined claim using evidence rather than trusting an operator's assertion. A document should state what is verifiable, by whom, against which evidence, and what the verification does not establish.

### Visibility mode

The access boundary applied to a process or record: public, participant- or member-visible, protected review, or private. Visibility and authority are related but not identical.

## W

### Wallet

The participant-controlled system for managing credentials, pseudonyms, keys, recovery methods, sessions, and authorizations. A wallet is not necessarily a financial wallet or public profile.

## Z

### Zero-knowledge proof

A cryptographic method that can prove a statement without revealing the underlying secret. For example, a system may prove that a credential satisfies an age rule without revealing the birth date. A zero-knowledge proof does not automatically solve issuer trust, device security, revocation, coercion, usability, or network metadata.
