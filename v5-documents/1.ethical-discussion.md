# A Universal Union: Ethical Discussion

**Status:** Conceptual ethical framework; not a final moral code, legal charter, or technical specification

**Primary readers:** Motivated general readers, policy readers, and contributors evaluating AUU's moral direction

## 1. Purpose

A civic system cannot be judged only by whether it runs, scales, verifies accounts, protects data, or counts votes correctly. It must also be judged by the environment it creates: who gains agency, who carries risk, which forms of power become easier to exercise, and which failures can be corrected.

A Universal Union is a proposal about power, speech, trust, privacy, responsibility, and collective action before it is a proposal about software. An identity rule carries assumptions about personhood. A voting rule carries assumptions about authority. A discussion rule carries assumptions about truth and disagreement. A record rule carries assumptions about exposure, accountability, memory, and forgiveness.

No single principle settles all of those questions. The same protection can serve freedom in one direction and domination in another.

| Capacity | What it can protect | What it can also enable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Privacy | Conscience, dissent, association, safety, and room to change. | Hidden power, concealed abuse, or unreviewable authority. |
| Visibility | Public accountability and correction. | Retaliation, surveillance, harassment, and permanent exposure. |
| Free expression | Discovery, testimony, dissent, and early warning. | Intimidation, recruitment, deception, and coordinated harm. |
| Local autonomy | Cultural difference, experimentation, and self-government. | Local domination, exclusion, and harm pushed across borders. |
| Civic memory | Continuity, evidence, learning, and resistance to revisionism. | Permanent punishment, dangerous disclosure, and loss of context. |
| Delegation | Participation without constant attention. | Concentrated influence and practical surrender. |
| Analytics | Navigation through complexity. | Biased interpretation, social mapping, and hidden algorithmic rule. |

The ethical task is not to choose one side of every row. It is to design relationships among the capacities so that each restrains the dangers of the others.

This document therefore has four jobs:

1. state AUU's core ethical commitments;
2. explain the view of society and power behind them;
3. apply them to the most difficult civic relationships; and
4. name the tensions that design alone cannot settle.

The [Feature Sets](./2.feature-sets.md) document defines functional requirements. The [Research and Development Strategy](./3.research-and-development-strategy.md) defines how uncertain mechanisms should be investigated and tested. This document does not convert an ethical preference into a claim that the system can already deliver it.

## 2. Core ethical commitments

### 2.1 Human dignity and agency

AUU begins from equal human dignity. A person must not be reduced to a data point, market unit, ideological token, source of engagement, or object to be governed.

Dignity requires more than polite treatment. It requires meaningful agency: room to think, speak, associate, dissent, participate, withdraw, change one's mind, challenge authority, and influence the choices that shape one's life. A platform that records participation while leaving people powerless has documented civic weakness, not repaired it. A vote that measures preference but has no accountable path to action may count people without giving them control.

Agency also concerns the menu of choices. If people can only select among packages already chosen by parties, institutions, administrators, donors, or platform owners, they may have formal input without meaningful authorship. AUU should help people originate issues, build alternatives, revise proposals, and organize around positions that do not fit a preselected binary.

No system can give every person control over every outcome. People conflict, resources are limited, and one person's choice can impose costs on another. The ethical requirement is narrower: people should have a fair path to understand, influence, contest, and revisit exercises of power that materially affect them.

### 2.2 Consent and bounded representation

Consent separates participation from extraction.

A system must not treat a person's identity, attention, association, reputation, ballot, labor, or authority as available merely because it can access them. One action must not silently authorize many others. Joining a group is not consent to every public statement. Speaking in one discussion is not permission to merge every context into a permanent profile. Holding a credential is not permission to publish the fact behind it.

Meaningful consent should be specific, understandable, proportionate, and revocable where revocation remains possible. Power, fear, dependence, exhaustion, and lack of alternatives can make a formal “yes” unreliable. AUU cannot determine the inner freedom of every choice, but it can avoid inflating limited agreement into broader exposure or authority.

Membership and representation must therefore remain separate. A person may belong to a union, church, workplace, neighborhood group, party, family, or federation without authorizing that body to speak for every belief or interest. When a collective claims to represent members, affected people, residents, workers, or another body, the claim needs a bounded source: a vote, delegation, charter, office, law, ratification, or other stated process.

### 2.3 Privacy for persons; accountability for power

Ordinary people need privacy to think, experiment, dissent, recover from error, and participate without becoming permanently exposed. Dissidents, whistleblowers, abuse survivors, workers, tenants, refugees, stigmatized minorities, and people under hostile institutions may face much greater risks from identification.

Privacy changes meaning when power is exercised over others. A person or organization making a private choice is not in the same position as a delegate, employer, landlord, platform, public office, collective, or federation claiming authority. The more consequential the power, the stronger the duty to show where it came from, what it permits, what it did, and how it can be challenged.

This is not a division between morally superior and inferior classes of people. Everyone retains basic rights and private personhood. Accountability duties scale with role, effect, knowledge, and power. A legally private actor can still exercise enormous social power; an employer, caregiver, household member, property owner, media owner, donor, or platform operator may shape other lives without holding public office.

The controlling rule is therefore:

> Protect people from unnecessary exposure. Do not let protected identity become unreviewable power.

Review does not always mean publication to everyone. AUU distinguishes public records, participant- or member-visible records, protected independent review, and private material. Secret ballots and sealed rosters may be legitimate. A protected review may be necessary for personal data, vulnerable groups, active safety matters, or restricted evidence. The process must still name who may inspect what, under which safeguards, and with which consequences.

### 2.4 Non-domination and peaceful recourse

Power cannot be eliminated. It can be made visible, limited, distributed, answerable, and easier to end.

A person is vulnerable to domination when another actor can arbitrarily shape their housing, work, care, identity, speech, access, reputation, or political participation and the person has no practical way to understand, contest, or escape that control. States can dominate. So can companies, landlords, families, parties, religious bodies, unions, platforms, movements, local communities, and informal leaders.

Rules and authority are sometimes necessary. The danger begins when authority becomes difficult to question, review, remove, or revise. AUU should make peaceful correction a normal part of civic life. Leaders can be replaced. Grants of authority can end. Decisions can be appealed under defined conditions. Evidence can reach a reviewer. People can build lawful alternatives without first destroying the existing order.

This is the ethical meaning of being **anti-necessity-of-violence**. The phrase does not mean passive obedience, rejection of resistance, or denial that conflicts can become violent. It means that a peaceful civic order has a duty to keep meaningful paths of correction open before people conclude that only force can make power respond.

Speech alone is not recourse. A person can complain, petition, march, or expose wrongdoing and remain entirely dependent on the target's willingness to act. AUU's aim is to connect public reasoning to organized, limited, reviewable capacity for action. It cannot guarantee justice, but it should not make helpless expression the only peaceful option.

### 2.5 Pluralism within a governed rights floor

AUU is intended for communities that disagree about politics, culture, religion, economy, institutional form, and the good life. It must not turn one ideology, country, governance method, or moral vocabulary into the required template for everyone.

Pluralism does not mean that any local rule becomes acceptable once a group votes for it. A majority cannot make domination, coerced membership, fraudulent representation, involuntary exposure, or the removal of basic human standing ethical merely by following local procedure. A rights floor exists to keep autonomy from becoming a shield for abuse.

The current project proposes a provisional floor. It begins with equal moral dignity, freedom of conscience, due process, and meaningful voice for affected people. It protects people from unnecessary exposure and from representation they did not freely grant. It also requires a path to challenge consequential power and, where exit can be made real, to leave abusive structures. This is a project commitment, not a claim that AUU already possesses universal legal authority or a complete theory of rights.

That distinction creates a governance obligation. Before any implementation can rely on a rights floor, it must publish:

- the charter or rule that states the floor;
- who adopted or ratified it;
- who may interpret it in a dispute;
- how reviewers are chosen and challenged;
- how the floor can be amended without letting a temporary majority erase it;
- which remedies or restrictions follow from a violation;
- how conflicts with law or another federation are handled; and
- what it means when a community rejects the floor.

Some protections may be enforced in software. Others depend on institutions, law, human judgment, or refusal to recognize an abusive authority claim. A community may use compatible open components while rejecting AUU's ethical commitments, but use of the code would not by itself establish conformity with AUU or recognition by another collective or federation.

The rights floor must therefore be explicit and governed. Invoking “basic rights” without naming an authority path would merely hide one of the most important powers in the system.

### 2.6 Proportional civic responsibility and accessible understanding

Civilization is produced partly through ordinary choices. Ignoring that fact turns every person into a spectator waiting for someone else to repair what everyone helps reproduce. But assigning equal responsibility to unequal people is also unjust.

A child, exhausted worker, disabled person, isolated elder, traumatized person, refugee, caregiver, public official, wealthy donor, and media owner do not have the same time, knowledge, safety, influence, or practical alternatives. Responsibility should scale with capacity, knowledge, power, consequence, and access.

People should make a sincere effort to understand the systems they support, the harms they normalize, and the repairs they can contribute. Institutions carry the matching duty to make understanding possible. A process cannot blame people for ignorance while hiding rules behind technical language, inaccessible interfaces, paywalls, poor translation, excessive time demands, or systems that require expert working memory.

This duty shapes AUU directly. Civic participation must be designed for disability, low literacy, limited bandwidth, language difference, cognitive overload, irregular availability, and the need for trusted assistance. Accessibility is not a feature added after the authority model is complete. If only highly educated, constantly available, technically skilled people can understand or use the process, they will acquire power through endurance.

The ethical demand is not that every person become a policy expert. It is that people should be able to understand what is being asked of them, what they are authorizing, what they are exposing, where their influence goes, and how to seek help or correction.

### 2.7 Memory, repair, and reversibility

People and institutions will make mistakes. A civic system must therefore preserve both memory and the possibility of repair.

Memory without repair can turn every error into permanent exile. Repair without memory can become denial, image management, or repeated harm without consequence. An honest record may show that harm occurred, a response followed, rules changed, restitution was attempted, and trust was partly restored—or that the attempt failed.

Different objects require different forms of reversibility. A person should be able to change a position while a process remains open. Delegation should be revocable under its stated rule. A role should expire or be reviewable. A completed vote needs procedural finality, but the decision may be reconsidered when new evidence, failed implementation, procedural error, coercion, or changed conditions justify it.

Records should be proportionate to consequence. A formal decision, public claim, delegation, moderation action, or exercise of office needs stronger memory than a private draft or casual reaction. Retention never creates a duty to keep dangerous content publicly available. Evidence may require redaction, restricted review, lawful deletion, or a minimal record that an item was removed and why.

### 2.8 Institutional humility

No author, expert, party, court, platform, model, government, or collective permanently solves the ethical problems of civilization. AUU must apply that uncertainty to itself.

The system should make its classifications, summaries, rankings, trust signals, rules, defaults, and maturity claims open to correction. It should record major design decisions and the objections that remain. It should be able to deprecate a mechanism without pretending the earlier confidence never existed.

Humility does not mean refusing to decide. It means naming assumptions, limits, and failure conditions before authority rests on them. A civic system that cannot admit error becomes another institution defending itself.

## 3. Why civic structure matters

### 3.1 Society shapes the choices made inside it

Individuals remain responsible for their actions, but no action occurs in a vacuum. People choose inside environments of law, scarcity, reward, punishment, habit, example, narrative, and unequal power. Schools, workplaces, markets, housing systems, media, platforms, families, courts, and governments teach people what succeeds and what costs too much.

If deception repeatedly produces advantage, deception becomes adaptive. If cruelty carries status, cruelty spreads. If cooperation is punished as weakness, cooperation retreats. If a platform rewards anger with reach and attention, anger becomes a practical way to be seen. None of this removes individual choice. It changes the pressures surrounding choice.

Institutions should therefore be judged partly by the conduct they select for. The ethical target is not diagnosis. AUU should not infer personality disorders, psychological types, or hidden moral character and use those labels to allocate civic rights, visibility, leadership, or trust. It should govern observable conduct and reviewable process: what an actor did, under which authority, with which evidence, how they responded to challenge, and whether repair occurred.

This is safer and more relevant. A society should not depend on leaders having good souls. It should make destructive conduct harder to hide and reward, and constructive conduct easier to coordinate and preserve.

### 3.2 Scattered awareness is not organized power

People often know that an institution is failing. Awareness may be widespread while action remains impossible. One person cannot expose the problem safely. Another is not believed. Many people agree but cannot find one another. A group forms but lacks authority, protection, resources, or an implementation path.

The missing bridge is organization.

That bridge can also be captured. Money, law, bureaucracy, media access, ownership, technical control, or force may allow a small organized veto to defeat a large scattered preference. Better information and wider participation help, but they do not automatically make a decision binding or implementation faithful.

AUU therefore treats civic agency as a sequence rather than a moment. People need ways to originate an issue, discover who is affected, test evidence, authorize a limited mandate, delegate action, record follow-through, challenge failure, repair harm, and coordinate across groups. A vote is one possible step in that sequence, not the whole of democracy.

### 3.3 Attention and language are fields of power

Legal freedom to speak does not guarantee a healthy environment for thought. Owners, advertisers, institutions, parties, platforms, editors, audiences, and ranking systems all affect what receives attention. This influence need not be centrally planned. Ordinary incentives can repeatedly favor outrage, certainty, humiliation, novelty, access, and group loyalty over patience, context, and correction.

Abundant speech can still produce intellectual enclosure. Important claims may be buried by volume, fragmented across incompatible vocabularies, or made exhausting to follow. Different groups may use the same word for different fears or different words for the same concern. Each group can then experience the other's language as proof of stupidity or malice.

Structured dialogue should begin with accurate disagreement. What does each claim mean to the person making it? Which facts, values, harms, definitions, and authority assumptions differ? What would each side need the other to understand before they could disagree fairly?

Translation does not require false unity. Some claims are deceptive. Some goals conflict. Some actions violate rights. Understanding the concern beneath a position does not require accepting the position or suspending accountability. It can slow the premature conversion of partial information into certainty about an entire person.

AUU should therefore help participants summarize an opposing claim fairly, locate the actual disagreement, preserve minority interpretations, and connect claims to evidence and challenge. It should not reward a faction merely for generating more volume, attention, or emotional intensity.

### 3.4 Financial resources must not become civic worth

Money is an essential coordination tool, and civic infrastructure itself requires funding, maintenance, accessibility work, incident response, and skilled labor. The ethical problem is not money's existence. It is allowing wealth to become the formal source of personhood, voting weight, public truth, or authority over the rules of civic legitimacy.

Systems built around token ownership, investment, advertising, donations, or paid reach can make capital function as voting power even without ordinary bribery. Wealth may also exert indirect influence by funding research, operators, campaigns, institutions, litigation, and public attention. No architecture can simply declare itself free of that pressure.

AUU should therefore separate formal civic authority from asset ownership wherever the context does not independently justify an economic stake. One person should not count as more human because they own more. A donor should not silently gain rule-making authority. A sponsor should not control what can be challenged. Funding arrangements, conflicts, operator incentives, and maintenance authority must be visible and reviewable.

This is a design and governance requirement, not a completed solution. The R&D program must investigate how infrastructure can be sustained without granting funders an unreviewable path to capture.

## 4. Applying the commitments

### 4.1 Membership, standing, advocacy, and representation

A civic process must not confuse the people present with everyone affected by its outcome.

Children, disabled people, prisoners, displaced people, undocumented residents, people without reliable internet, people under surveillance, and people excluded by time, language, literacy, documentation, or fear may be absent from a process that changes their lives. Their absence does not erase their moral standing.

At the same time, absence cannot become a blank cheque for someone else to claim their authority. AUU must distinguish several relationships:

| Relationship | What it permits | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal speech | A person states their own view. | That anyone else agrees. |
| Advocacy | A person raises another group's possible interests or harms. | That the group authorized the advocate. |
| Affected standing | A person or group is entitled to be heard or to challenge because they may bear a consequence. | Automatic membership or decision power. |
| Expertise or witness standing | A person submits relevant knowledge or direct evidence. | Authority to choose the outcome. |
| Membership | A person belongs under a collective's stated rule. | Consent to every decision or public claim. |
| Guardianship or office | A role carries duties under a stated legal, social, or institutional source. | Unlimited authority or freedom from review. |
| Delegation or mandate | A defined source granted authority for a defined purpose. | Authority outside that scope or after expiry. |
| Representation | An actor may speak or act for others under a reviewable basis. | That every represented person agrees. |

This table resolves an apparent conflict in the earlier drafts. Participation may build knowledge, reputation, relationships, or delegated authority. It is not the sole source of standing. A person can be affected before participating, and some people cannot safely participate at all.

The more vulnerable, absent, or unable to contest representation a person is, the stronger the duty to limit and review claims made on their behalf. A process should ask: who is speaking, under what relationship, for which action, with which evidence, until when, and how can the claim be challenged?

### 4.2 Pseudonymity, anonymity, and lawful power

Pseudonymity is necessary for free conscience and association. A person should not have to attach their legal identity and every context of their life to each question, vote, group, or unpopular belief.

Protection must be described honestly. AUU may reduce public linkability, separate contexts, minimize stored identity data, protect rosters, use privacy-preserving eligibility checks, and route sensitive traffic through stronger privacy systems. None of those steps establishes universal anonymity.

Different observers may possess different information. A public reader may see only a pseudonym. A credential issuer may know who received a credential. An operator may see logs or timing. A network provider may see connections. A hostile member may recognize writing or local facts. An employer or state may seize a device. Recovery processes can reconnect identities that ordinary participation keeps apart.

Privacy claims must therefore name a threat model: what is protected, from whom, under which assumptions, and which links remain possible. “The platform does not know who you are” is not acceptable unless the architecture and operations actually make that statement true against a named observer.

Law enforcement should receive no hidden identity backdoor or special civic authority merely because it is law enforcement. Officers, regulators, journalists, researchers, watchdogs, and ordinary participants may submit evidence or challenges under the same platform rules unless a lawful external authority changes the situation.

AUU cannot promise immunity from law. Operators may receive court orders or other legal demands, and devices, networks, credential issuers, or outside conduct may identify people. The ethical duties are to collect as little sensitive information as the function allows, publish what kinds of information exist, restrict access, document requests where lawful, challenge improper demands where possible, and never advertise protection that the system has not demonstrated.

### 4.3 Expression, abuse, coordinated harm, and moderation

Free expression matters partly because speech makes grievance, confusion, testimony, hostility, and emerging danger visible while they can still be answered. Hiding every disturbing idea can protect institutions from criticism and push people into spaces where the idea encounters only reinforcement.

Permission alone is not a complete response. Speech can target, intimidate, deceive, recruit, harass, expose, or help coordinate harm. AUU needs distinctions based on conduct and consequence rather than a permanent ideological authority.

| Kind of activity | Preferred civic response |
| --- | --- |
| Unpopular, offensive, or mistaken expression | Counterargument, context, evidence, translation, user controls, and ordinary community rules. |
| Repeated targeting, harassment, manipulation, or process sabotage | Protective controls, moderation, warnings, rate limits, review, and appeal. |
| Credible threats, doxxing, intimate material, child sexual abuse material, or evidence tied to serious harm | Immediate safety handling, restricted evidence, lawful escalation where applicable, removal or access limits, and a reviewable procedural record that does not redistribute the material. |
| Disputed classification | A defined challenge and appeal path with protection against retaliation and repeated abuse. |

The boundaries will remain difficult. Governments may call dissent dangerous. Companies may call reputational risk harm. Factions may call disagreement violence. Abusive groups may call intimidation speech. No central actor should be trusted to draw every boundary permanently and invisibly.

This does not require one universal moderation code. It requires visible rules, scoped community authority, minimum safety duties, records proportionate to the action, and review of the moderators themselves. Moderation is governance. People affected by it should be able to understand what rule was applied, who or what applied it, which evidence can safely be reviewed, and how to appeal.

Preserving a procedural record is not the same as preserving harmful content in public. A record may say that material was removed under a rule, reviewed by a named role, referred to another process, or later restored. The dangerous material itself may need to remain inaccessible, redacted, lawfully deleted, or available only under protected review.

### 4.4 Public challenge without permanent procedural warfare

Authority-bearing claims need a meaningful way to be challenged. A collective should not become the only court allowed to hear its own appeal. But a challenge mechanism can itself become a weapon: opponents may flood every decision, repeat rejected claims, force vulnerable people to relive harm, or make leadership spend forever answering bad-faith objections.

The ethical requirement is a challenge capability, not one mandatory interface layout. A separate challenge lane may protect member deliberation in one context; a shared forum may work in another. Either design should make the lifecycle clear.

A usable challenge process should state:

1. **Target:** the exact claim, action, rule, record, authority, or consequence being challenged.
2. **Standing:** whether the challenger is a member, affected person, expert, witness, advocate, observer, or another actor.
3. **Basis:** the evidence, reasoning, right, procedure, or risk being raised.
4. **Threshold:** what receives a response and what may be combined, redirected, limited, or dismissed as irrelevant or abusive.
5. **Status:** for example, received, under review, answered, resolved, unresolved, dismissed, withdrawn, or appealed.
6. **Response duty:** who must answer, by when, and what happens if they do not.
7. **Review:** who determines status or remedy and how that reviewer is held accountable.
8. **Record:** what remains attached, for how long, under which visibility mode, with which safety exceptions.

A challenge is not automatically correct and does not automatically stop a decision. A dismissal is not permission to erase criticism. The design must preserve serious unresolved objections while giving processes a lawful way to reach closure.

### 4.5 Distributed power and federation

Centralized and distributed systems fail differently.

A central authority may coordinate quickly and apply common rules. If captured, it can impose the failure across everyone beneath it. A distributed system makes one total capture harder, but can produce local abuse, inconsistent protections, duplicated work, weak coordination, and responsibility scattered until no one can be held answerable.

AUU therefore favors **bounded decentralization**: local autonomy joined to rights floors, reviewable authority, records, challenge, and coordination across shared problems. The purpose is not to make hierarchy impossible. Some communities and institutions will choose hierarchies, councils, assemblies, offices, consensus, elected representatives, or mixtures. The purpose is to keep any structure from becoming unchallengeable merely because it is local, traditional, technical, or decentralized.

Federation should let collectives cooperate without becoming one uniform body. A federation must state who its members are, what each member recognizes, how joint authority is formed, how dissent is preserved, how a member leaves, and what happens when local decisions produce effects beyond local boundaries.

Recognition is not automatic. One collective may accept another's credential, delegate, decision, or authority; another may not. Software standards can carry records and proofs between systems. They cannot force political, moral, or legal acceptance. Every document that uses *recognized* must identify the actor or process doing the recognizing.

Exit is valuable but not always enough. A person may lack money, safety, mobility, legal status, alternative care, or somewhere else to go. Some harms cross property lines, jurisdictions, generations, or ecosystems. Local experimentation is strongest where effects can be contained and nonparticipants are not forced to pay hidden costs.

### 4.6 AUU's own interpretive power

AUU may need summaries, search, classification, argument maps, trust-sensitive views, disagreement maps, manipulation warnings, and other analytic tools. Without help navigating complexity, practical power moves toward the people with the most time, education, technical skill, staff, or emotional endurance.

Interpretive help creates its own authority. A summary can decide what appears important. A category can frame intent. A warning can damage reputation before review. A cluster can turn a temporary pattern into an apparent faction. A confidence score can make uncertain judgment look scientific.

AUU must therefore separate records from interpretations.

- A summary is not the discussion.
- A trust-sensitive view is not the public will.
- A warning is not a verdict.
- A cluster is not a community.
- A model output is not civic truth.

For low-stakes navigation, correction may be lightweight. When an interpretation shapes a vote, authority claim, moderation action, reputation consequence, or public record, stronger safeguards are required. The output should link to its sources, state uncertainty and missing data, preserve competing summaries or minority reports, and provide a visible correction path before participants are expected to rely on it.

An audit trail alone is insufficient if a false summary has already directed everyone's attention. High-consequence interpretations may require human review, temporary or provisional labels, notice to the people summarized, and the ability to delay formal use while a material dispute is examined.

AUU should use interpretation to help people reason, not to become an invisible governor of what they are able to see.

## 5. Ethical commitments as design tests

The commitments above become useful when they can reject a design.

| Commitment | A design fails when… |
| --- | --- |
| Dignity and agency | Participation is recorded but people cannot shape options, understand consequences, or contest power. |
| Consent | One action silently authorizes wider exposure, representation, analysis, or control. |
| Privacy for persons | The system collects or reveals more identity, association, ballot, or credential information than the function needs. |
| Accountability for power | A consequential claim or decision has no reviewable source, scope, rule, record, or challenge path. |
| Non-domination | An actor can exercise durable power while remaining practically impossible to understand, remove, constrain, or appeal. |
| Peaceful recourse | People may object but have no credible path from evidence and organization to correction. |
| Pluralism | One governance form or worldview is imposed where difference could safely remain. |
| Rights floor | Local autonomy or majority choice is used to erase basic protection or standing. |
| Proportional responsibility | The process assigns equal burdens while ignoring differences in capacity, access, power, or consequence. |
| Accessibility | Only highly literate, technically skilled, continuously available, or well-resourced participants can understand and use the process. |
| Memory and repair | Records either disappear when power changes hands or trap people forever without a route to correction. |
| Institutional humility | A model, moderator, maintainer, reviewer, or rule cannot be challenged or revised. |
| Anti-capture | Wealth, ownership, sponsorship, administration, or technical control silently becomes civic authority. |

Passing one test does not excuse failure on another. A private system may still hide power. A transparent system may still expose vulnerable people. A verifiable vote may still use unfair eligibility. A decentralized network may still contain local domination.

## 6. Questions ethics cannot answer alone

The following questions require governance, research, law, implementation, or all four. Leaving them open is more honest than solving them through confident prose.

| Question | What a responsible next step must produce |
| --- | --- |
| Who governs the rights floor? | A charter, ratification source, interpreter, amendment rule, reviewer, remedy, federation-conflict rule, and conformity boundary. |
| Who recognizes an authority claim? | A named recognizer, acceptance criteria, duration, revocation rule, and appeal path. |
| How does protected accountability work? | Visibility rules, reviewer selection, access safeguards, public metadata, findings, appeal, and misuse consequences. |
| What may a voting receipt prove? | A threat model and validated construction that separates submission, inclusion, tally verification, delegation status, and proof of a secret choice. |
| How are conflicting representatives handled? | Rules for competing claims, legal office, member consent, guardianship, overlapping mandates, and expiry. |
| How are dangerous records handled? | Retention, redaction, restricted review, lawful deletion, legal hold, victim safety, and a non-distributive procedural record. |
| How is challenge prevented from becoming harassment? | Standing labels, response thresholds, combination of duplicates, time limits, safety controls, closure, and appeal. |
| How is the infrastructure funded? | A maintenance and funding model with conflict disclosure, limits on sponsor control, operational responsibility, and continuity planning. |
| How are inaccessible processes rejected? | Defined users, tasks, assistive conditions, comprehension measures, failure thresholds, and a required redesign path. |
| Who governs AUU itself? | Named decision rights over definitions, protocols, maturity labels, moderation, incidents, releases, federation standards, and deprecation. |

The R&D strategy assigns these questions to evidence gates. No feature or pilot should quietly answer them through an implementation default.

## 7. Closing principle

Civilization is shaped by the conduct its environments make easier, safer, more profitable, more visible, and more durable. Better systems cannot make people good. Poor systems can still make manipulation easier to reward, power easier to hide, dissent more dangerous, and repair harder to reach.

AUU's ethical aim is therefore modest in claim but demanding in practice. Protect people from unnecessary exposure. Refuse to let privacy hide consequential power. Keep peaceful correction reachable. Preserve difference without abandoning basic protections. Connect authority to a source and a limit. Remember enough to learn. Remain humble enough to be corrected by the people living with the result.

The ethical work is not finished when the document ends. A civic system worthy of trust must make its own power answerable too.
