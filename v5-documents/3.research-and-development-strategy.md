# A Universal Union: Research and Development Strategy

**Status:** Proposed development program; no component is production-approved by this document

**Primary readers:** Researchers, maintainers, designers, implementers, reviewers, pilot partners, and funders evaluating the work required

## 1. Purpose and current posture

The mission explains why AUU is being proposed. The ethics document defines values and limits. The feature and social-structure documents define intended civic functions. This document answers a different question:

> What work, evidence, decisions, and safeguards are required before any part of AUU can be trusted for a particular use?

AUU is currently a conceptual and architectural project. Broad functional coverage is not technical maturity. A feature remains a concept or research target until it has a bounded problem statement, evidence review, specification, implementation, adversarial tests, accessibility evidence, operational plan, and appropriate pilot record.

The strategy follows five rules:

1. **Build the smallest artifact that can disprove an assumption.** A narrow evaluator or state machine may be more useful than an impressive application.
2. **Test integrations, not only components.** Privacy, verification, coercion, records, authority, and usability often conflict at their boundaries.
3. **Match evidence to consequence.** An advisory poll and a binding election do not require the same assurance.
4. **Let findings move work backward.** A failed pilot or new threat returns a specification to research or redesign.
5. **Do not use vulnerable people as the first realistic test of a safety claim.** Simulations, expert review, and lower-risk users come first.

## 2. Program governance

The old strategy assigned maturity without naming who could make that judgment. A credible program must publish its decision rights before it publishes readiness claims.

### 2.1 Required roles

One person may hold several roles during early research, but the overlap must be disclosed. High-consequence approval requires independence.

| Role | Responsibility | Must not approve alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Project maintainer | Maintains the document set, issue register, decision records, repository, and roadmap. | A production or high-risk safety claim they designed or implemented. |
| Workstream owner | Defines questions, artifacts, tests, dependencies, and evidence for one area. | Their own maturity promotion beyond prototype. |
| Research reviewer | Evaluates source quality, competing evidence, and whether conclusions follow. | A domain where they have a material conflict without disclosure and independent review. |
| Security and privacy reviewer | Reviews threats, data flows, identity bridges, cryptography, network assumptions, and operations. | High-risk deployment without a second independent review appropriate to the threat. |
| Governance reviewer | Reviews authority, standing, rights, representation, challenge, federation, and capture. | The legitimacy of a process in which they hold unreviewable authority. |
| Accessibility and UX reviewer | Reviews participant tasks, comprehension, disability access, language, cognitive load, and assistance. | A claim that “ordinary users understand” without participant evidence. |
| Safety and moderation reviewer | Reviews abuse, dangerous content, appeals, reviewer welfare, and misuse of safeguards. | A safety rule that lacks an appeal or oversight path. |
| Legal and data reviewer | Reviews applicable law, retention, deletion, children, jurisdiction, evidence, procurement, and institutional duties. | Universal legal compliance. Every deployment still needs its jurisdictional review. |
| Pilot lead | Runs consent, recruitment, training, incident response, withdrawal, data handling, and reporting. | A pilot involving risks they can conceal or findings they can suppress. |
| Deployment decision body | Accepts the complete evidence package for a defined use and records the decision, objections, limits, and expiry. | Undefined or universal “production readiness.” Approval applies only to a named use and threat model. |

Before a consequential pilot, the project must identify real people or accountable organizations for the relevant roles. A table of empty titles is not governance.

### 2.2 Decision records and open objections

Major choices require a stable research or architecture decision record. The issue tracker may host discussion, but a decision must not remain buried in a thread.

A decision record should state:

- the question and decision;
- owner and date;
- alternatives considered;
- evidence and source status;
- assumptions and threat model;
- privacy, accessibility, governance, and operational effects;
- objections and minority position;
- implementation and migration cost;
- maturity effect;
- review or expiry date; and
- evidence that would reopen or reverse it.

Rejected approaches should remain searchable with the reason for rejection. This reduces the chance that the project repeats the same unsafe idea after contributors change.

### 2.3 Claims and assurance register

The project should maintain a register of claims that use words such as *anonymous*, *secure*, *private*, *verifiable*, *coercion-resistant*, *accessible*, *representative*, *auditable*, or *production-ready*. Each entry should name the property, observer or threat, evidence, limit, maturity, owner, and revisit condition.

No marketing or public documentation should make a stronger claim than the register supports.

## 3. Workstreams and dependencies

The program has a critical path. Some work can proceed in parallel; high-consequence work cannot skip its foundations.

### 3.1 Dependency table

| ID | Workstream | Produces | Depends on | Unlocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| W0 | Project governance and ethics | Decision authority, rights-floor work, claim labels, review rules, conflicts, funding boundaries. | Current documents and contributor agreement. | Every maturity and deployment decision. |
| W1 | Shared civic semantics | Actor types, standing, authority, recognition, visibility, record, challenge, and lifecycle schemas. | W0. | Identity interfaces, deliberation, collectives, delegation, voting, federation. |
| W2 | Accessibility and participant understanding | User groups, task models, plain-language patterns, disability requirements, translation, low-bandwidth and assistance rules, test plans. | Begins with W0; tests every later artifact. | Trustworthy interfaces and pilot acceptance criteria. |
| W3 | Identity, eligibility, security, recovery, and network exposure | Threat models, wallet boundaries, eligibility interface, security policy, recovery states, metadata model, candidate privacy paths. | W0–W2 and W1 actor/visibility semantics. | Credential-gated processes, protected participation, voting, roles, delegation. |
| W4 | Deliberation, records, challenge, and follow-through | Issue lifecycle, source-linked synthesis, challenge states, record visibility, moderation safety, action tracking. | W0–W2 and W1. | Low-risk pilots, decision context, analytics, collective accountability. |
| W5 | Authority, collectives, roles, and delegation | Authority evaluator, governance configuration, representation claims, collective conflicts, delegation and revocation states. | W1, W3 eligibility/recovery, and W4 records/challenge. | Formal collective governance, voting, federation. |
| W6 | Voting and coercion-resistant verification | Election requirements, eligibility and authority integration, ballot model, proof types, coercion analysis, tally and audit tests. | W1, W3, W4, and W5. | Binding-vote simulations; never binding use by itself. |
| W7 | Analytics and interpretive accountability | Summary correction, uncertainty, privacy thresholds, manipulation tests, source linkage, appeals. | W2, W4 records/correction, and W3 privacy model. | Decision-support tests and large-scale navigation. |
| W8 | Federation and interoperability | Recognition, member authority, dissent, exit, competing claims, data exchange, migration, and external acceptance rules. | W5 plus W4 records and W6 if federation votes. | Inter-collective pilots and migration tests. |
| W9 | Operations, data lifecycle, law, funding, and maintenance | Deployment model, retention and deletion, incident response, moderation operations, legal reviews, sustainability and capture controls. | Starts with W0 and constrains all workstreams. | Any sustained pilot or deployment. |

### 3.2 Critical path

The minimum route to a low-risk, non-binding pilot is:

**W0 governance → W1 shared semantics → W2 accessible task design + W4 deliberation/record prototype → adversarial and usability review → pilot.**

The minimum route to a binding-vote simulation is longer:

**W0 → W1 → W2 → W3 identity and recovery → W4 records and challenge → W5 authority → W6 voting → W9 operations → independent reviews → simulation.**

Federation follows tested collective authority, not the other way around. Analytics that shape decisions follow a correction and privacy model. High-risk protected participation follows network, device, operator, recovery, legal, and incident-response review; a pseudonym interface alone cannot unlock it.

### 3.3 Parallel work

The following work should begin early and continue across the program:

- glossary and plain-language review;
- accessibility and cognitive-load research;
- privacy and threat modeling;
- human-subject and pilot ethics;
- data and legal analysis;
- funding and operator-capture analysis;
- source review across technical and social disciplines; and
- maintenance of decisions, objections, incidents, and deprecated approaches.

These are not late audits. Findings can block or redesign any workstream.

## 4. Evidence package for every consequential artifact

A feature should not advance because a prototype “works.” Its owner must produce an evidence package proportionate to the intended use.

| Evidence item | Required question |
| --- | --- |
| Problem statement | Which exact civic failure and actors are in scope? |
| Claim type and maturity | Is this a value, requirement, hypothesis, candidate, prototype result, or demonstrated property? |
| User and authority model | Who uses it, who is affected, and what authority could it create or remove? |
| Data-flow and visibility model | What is collected, derived, stored, shared, retained, deleted, or available through protected review? |
| Threat model | Which assets, adversaries, capabilities, assumptions, and harms matter? |
| Evidence review | What do standards, studies, deployments, and competing approaches establish or fail to establish? |
| Specification | What are the objects, states, interfaces, rules, errors, and invariants? |
| Accessibility task plan | Can intended users understand and complete the consequential tasks under relevant conditions? |
| Reference artifact | What small implementation, schema, evaluator, state machine, or flow tests the specification? |
| Adversarial tests | How can users, institutions, operators, attackers, and safeguards themselves abuse it? |
| Integration tests | Which neighboring properties conflict or fail when combined? |
| Residual risks | What remains possible, who bears the risk, and how does the use limit respond? |
| Operations | Who maintains it, handles incidents and appeals, funds it, updates it, and ends it safely? |
| Review record | Which independent reviewers accepted or objected, for which use, until what date? |

Acceptance criteria must be measurable. “Users understand,” “privacy is preserved,” or “the system resists abuse” is not enough. Each test must name participants or adversaries, tasks, environment, measure, threshold, failure handling, and reason the threshold is appropriate.

## 5. Workstream questions and first artifacts

### 5.1 W0–W2: governance, semantics, and accessibility

The first program artifacts should be civic before they are cryptographic.

**Required artifacts:**

1. a project-governance charter for decisions and maturity labels;
2. a provisional rights-floor governance proposal;
3. the controlled glossary and actor/standing/authority model;
4. the four visibility modes and safe-record rules;
5. a challenge and appeal state model;
6. participant journeys for low-literacy, screen-reader, keyboard-only, mobile, low-bandwidth, translated, cognitively overloaded, and assisted use; and
7. a comprehension-test template for authority, privacy, records, delegation, and irreversible actions.

**Gate:** No high-consequence artifact advances while the actors, authority, visibility, or intended participant task remain undefined.

### 5.2 W3: identity, eligibility, security, recovery, and network exposure

**Core questions:**

- How can one-person or eligibility rules resist duplicate use without forcing legal identity into every civic context?
- What can the participant, issuer, verifier, operator, network observer, insider, and device attacker learn?
- How are wallet access, pseudonym access, sessions, and sensitive action authorization separated?
- How are credentials expired or revoked without live identity bridges where possible?
- How does a rightful participant recover without giving attackers an easier path or erasing civic history?
- Which network protections reduce which observations, and what do they fail to hide?

**First artifacts:** an eligibility-request interface; wallet and session threat model; security-control policy; recovery and revocation state machine; metadata inventory; and a network-exposure test adapter.

**Gate:** A working credential demo does not unlock high-stakes use until correlation, exclusion, coercion, recovery, issuer, operator, device, and network risks are tested together.

### 5.3 W4: deliberation, records, challenge, moderation, and follow-through

**Core questions:**

- Can an issue move from exploration to proposal and decision without losing source context?
- Can summaries be corrected before they distort a consequential choice?
- Can members deliberate without outsiders taking control while affected outsiders retain meaningful challenge?
- Can challenges reach closure without being erased or turning into permanent harassment?
- Can dangerous evidence be reviewed without public redistribution?
- Can authorized action be followed into the world without treating uploaded documents as truth?

**First artifacts:** issue lifecycle; source-linked summary and correction model; challenge state machine; safe record and deletion model; moderation and appeal log; and follow-through status schema.

**Gate:** A discussion interface does not unlock authority claims until records, standing, correction, moderation, and challenge are understandable and testable.

### 5.4 W5: authority, collectives, roles, and delegation

**Core questions:**

- Can the system explain why an actor may perform an action, not merely whether a permission bit is set?
- How are membership, consent, office, standing, delegation, and representation kept distinct?
- What happens when two collectives claim the same institution or constituency?
- How does externally created authority enter, change, expire, and remain challengeable?
- How are delegation conflicts, concentration, onward delegation, revocation, and coercion handled?

**First artifacts:** authority-scope evaluator; collective configuration schema; public-claim contract; competing-claim model; role and office model; and delegation state machine.

**Gate:** Collective or delegate authority remains advisory until source, scope, recognition, conflict handling, records, and revocation have been tested with realistic users.

### 5.5 W6: voting and coercion-resistant verification

Voting research must begin with a use and threat model, not a preferred ledger or cryptographic primitive.

**Core questions:**

- Who is eligible and who recognizes the outcome?
- Which ballot and roster information must remain secret from which observers?
- What may a voter verify without gaining transferable proof of a protected choice or association?
- How are inclusion, tally correctness, authority, coercion, delegation, recovery, malicious clients, and operator compromise tested together?
- What happens when a ballot can be changed, a credential is revoked, authority conflicts, or the voting device is hostile?

**First artifacts:** receipt taxonomy; election requirements profile; coercion scenarios; malicious-client tests; small end-to-end voting lab; and a complete public election record for a simulated non-binding process.

**Gate:** No binding-use claim follows from a successful vote count. Election and coercion specialists, accessibility evidence, legal review, operational readiness, and an independent evidence package remain necessary.

### 5.6 W7: analytics and interpretive accountability

**Core questions:**

- Can a reader trace a summary, cluster, warning, or map back to sources?
- How are minority views, missing data, model disagreement, privacy suppression, and uncertainty shown?
- Can a participant correct material misrepresentation before an output shapes authority?
- Can manipulation detection distinguish hidden sponsorship from legitimate organized participation?
- Do combined outputs expose people or associations even when each output seems safe?

**First artifacts:** source-linked synthesis; competing-summary workflow; uncertainty schema; small-group privacy tests; model-challenge process; and adversarial datasets for coordinated behavior and false positives.

**Gate:** An analytic output cannot affect eligibility, moderation, reputation, or a formal decision until correction, privacy, bias, and appeal evidence match that consequence.

### 5.7 W8–W9: federation, operations, law, funding, and maintenance

**Federation questions:** Who recognizes whom? Which member authorization becomes federation authority? How are dissent, competing claims, overlapping jurisdiction, exit, and data migration preserved? What does technical interoperability fail to transfer?

**Operational questions:** Who runs services, issues updates, reviews protected evidence, moderates dangerous material, handles incidents, pays recurring costs, replaces failed operators, and shuts a system down safely? Which jurisdictions and child-user rules apply? What can be deleted, exported, migrated, legally held, or restored?

**Funding questions:** Which actors fund development and operation? What influence comes with money, contracts, infrastructure, staff, or access? How are conflicts disclosed? Which rule prevents a funder, host, credential issuer, app store, or maintainer from acquiring hidden civic authority?

**First artifacts:** federation recognition and conflict model; interoperability acceptance contract; deployment data map; incident and appeal runbooks; maintenance and end-of-life plan; legal issue register; and funding/capture policy.

**Gate:** No sustained or high-risk pilot begins without named operational owners, incident response, data duties, participant support, and a credible plan for continuity and exit.

## 6. Maturity is multidimensional

A feature is not mature merely because its code runs. AUU must track at least four dimensions separately:

1. **Conceptual maturity:** The purpose, terms, actors, authority, assumptions, and failure conditions are explicit.
2. **Technical maturity:** The implementation behaves as specified under normal, adverse, and recovery conditions.
3. **Human maturity:** Intended users can understand and use it, including people with disabilities, limited literacy, limited time, older devices, and unreliable connections.
4. **Operational maturity:** Named people and institutions can deploy, support, govern, fund, audit, repair, and retire it lawfully.

The overall maturity of a capability is the lowest of these four scores. A polished interface cannot raise an untested governance model to pilot readiness. Strong cryptography cannot compensate for an unsafe recovery process. A successful small pilot cannot establish behavior at national scale.

### 6.1 Maturity stages

| Stage | What has been established | What must not yet be claimed |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | The problem and affected people have been identified. | That AUU has an answer. |
| **Requirements** | Actors, authority, risks, accessibility needs, and testable requirements are documented. | That requirements are feasible or sufficient. |
| **Exploratory prototype** | One or more ideas can be exercised in a lab. | Security, usability, interoperability, or real-world fitness. |
| **Adversarial prototype** | Threats, misuse, recovery, and failure paths have been tested by people other than the builders. | Readiness for consequential use. |
| **Limited pilot** | A bounded, reversible, supported use has produced field evidence. | Fitness outside the tested people, setting, scale, and authority. |
| **Operational candidate** | Independent review, deployment controls, incident handling, accessibility, maintenance, and exit have been demonstrated. | Universal fitness or permanent safety. |
| **Qualified deployment** | A named authority has accepted a documented version for a defined use, population, jurisdiction, and period. | Legitimacy or safety outside that qualification. |

Every public status page should show the stage and the missing evidence. Terms such as *production-ready*, *secure*, *anonymous*, *accessible*, and *verified* require an attached definition and evidence package.

### 6.2 Gates are decisions, not optimism

Each gate must name:

- the decision and its scope;
- the person or body authorized to make it;
- the evidence reviewed;
- unresolved objections and minority reports;
- conditions, expiry, and monitoring;
- rollback and participant-remedy procedures; and
- the record through which the decision can be challenged.

A gate may produce **advance**, **revise**, **pause**, **narrow**, or **stop**. Failure to advance is useful evidence, not an embarrassment to conceal.

## 7. Evaluation framework

Testing should begin with the claim AUU wants to make, then ask what evidence could disprove it. Metrics selected after results are known are weak evidence.

### 7.1 Common evaluation questions

| Area | Questions | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding | Can a participant explain what happened, who had authority, what data was visible, and how to object? | Teach-back interviews, task observation, misconception log. |
| Accessibility | Can people with varied disabilities, literacy, language, devices, connections, and time constraints complete the same civic task? | Expert audit, assistive-technology tests, low-bandwidth tests, participant research. |
| Deliberation | Are relevant claims, reasons, uncertainty, and affected perspectives easier to find without manufacturing consensus? | Traced decision records, missed-view analysis, correction rate, qualitative review. |
| Authority | Can a reader distinguish advice, preference, delegation, formal authorization, action, and recognition? | Scope-reconstruction tests and authority-conflict scenarios. |
| Privacy | What can an observer, operator, participant, coerced user, compromised client, or data combiner infer? | Threat model, privacy tests, metadata analysis, red-team reports. |
| Security | Can unauthorized actors alter eligibility, authority, records, tallies, roles, or software without detection and recovery? | Penetration tests, protocol analysis, supply-chain review, recovery exercises. |
| Fairness | Who benefits, who carries cost, who is absent, and whose errors are harder to repair? | Distributional analysis, recruitment audit, subgroup error review. |
| Challenge and repair | Can a person raise a reasoned challenge, receive a timely response, appeal, and obtain repair? | Case traces, time-to-response, remedy quality, repeat-failure analysis. |
| Operations | Can the service survive staff changes, funding gaps, incidents, legal demands, migrations, and shutdown? | Runbooks, drills, financial model, succession and exit tests. |

Counts alone do not answer these questions. Participation volume can rise while comprehension, representation, or legitimacy falls. AUU therefore needs both quantitative measures and close reading of what participants experienced.

### 7.2 Comprehension as a release criterion

AUU's central usability test is not “Did the user click the correct button?” It is “Did the user form an accurate enough model to act knowingly?”

For each consequential flow, representative participants should be able to answer, in their own words:

1. What am I being asked to do?
2. Who will see my action and related metadata?
3. What will my action affect?
4. Who has authority to recognize the result?
5. Can I change or withdraw it, and until when?
6. How do I challenge an error or abuse?
7. What does the system still not guarantee?

Misunderstandings must be logged by severity. A common misunderstanding about privacy, authority, consent, eligibility, finality, or remedy blocks release until the design changes or the use is narrowed.

### 7.3 Precommitted success, failure, and stop conditions

Before a test or pilot begins, its plan should state:

- the hypotheses and important alternatives;
- participant population and important exclusions;
- measures and qualitative methods;
- acceptable and unacceptable outcomes;
- safety monitoring and stop authority;
- how missing data and withdrawals will be handled;
- which results may be generalized, if any; and
- where the protocol, changes, results, and dissent will be recorded.

Stopping conditions should include serious harm, unexpected exposure, inability to provide promised support, evidence that users misunderstand consequential effects, loss of a required operator, or discovery that the pilot lacks authority it claimed to possess.

## 8. Prototype and pilot ladder

Consequences should increase only as evidence increases.

| Level | Typical activity | Permitted consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper and facilitated simulation** | Role-play the civic cycle, authority disputes, and failure cases. | No platform-mediated civic consequence. |
| **Isolated lab** | Exercise components with synthetic or volunteer data. | Research learning only. |
| **Integrated sandbox** | Run the full cycle with test identities, records, and attacks. | No external authority; reversible test outcomes. |
| **Non-binding live pilot** | Support a real community question while another valid process retains authority. | Advice or learning, clearly labelled. |
| **Bounded consequential pilot** | Support a narrow decision with named authority, safeguards, alternatives, and remedy. | Only the declared, reversible scope. |
| **Federated pilot** | Test cross-collective recognition, dissent, exit, and data movement. | Only after each participating collective and the federation relationship pass earlier gates. |

### 8.1 Selecting pilots

An attractive partner or large audience does not make a good first pilot. Prefer a setting with:

- a real but bounded problem;
- a population that can be described without pretending every affected person is already represented;
- a cooperative decision authority and an independent challenge path;
- lower consequences and a credible fallback process;
- enough disagreement to test the model without inviting irreversible harm;
- support for accessibility, recruitment, moderation, and participant questions;
- permission to publish useful evidence, including failure; and
- no dependence on AUU for an essential benefit, legal right, employment decision, immigration status, medical care, or emergency response.

Initial pilots should not involve public-office elections, criminal adjudication, compulsory identity systems, allocation of essential services, or populations unable to decline without penalty.

### 8.2 Participant protection

Pilot participants need a plain-language explanation of purpose, authority, data use, visibility, risks, compensation, withdrawal, support, and complaint routes. Consent to research is separate from membership in a collective and separate again from consent to a decision.

Recruitment must look for people a convenient sample omits. Compensation should recognize time and expertise without becoming coercive. Where participants depend on an institution running the pilot, an independent contact and a non-digital route to withdraw or complain are especially important.

Children, dependent adults, people facing retaliation, and people whose legal or safety status could be exposed require specific review. “Public data” is not an automatic ethical permission to collect, combine, infer, or republish it.

### 8.3 Repair after a pilot

A pilot is not complete when data collection ends. The team must:

- resolve active participant harms and access requests;
- communicate results and limits in accessible language;
- publish incidents, material protocol changes, negative results, and unresolved objections where safe;
- state which claims changed;
- delete, retain, or transfer data according to the declared plan;
- retire unsupported services and credentials safely; and
- record what a later project must not assume from the pilot.

## 9. Architecture and reference artifacts

AUU should not begin by choosing a single permanent stack. It should begin with stable civic meanings and replaceable implementations.

### 9.1 Stable contracts, replaceable components

The shared contracts should define:

- actor, role, membership, eligibility, standing, authority, and recognition;
- issue, proposal, evidence, objection, decision rule, mandate, delegation, action, outcome, challenge, and repair;
- visibility, retention, correction, provenance, and export;
- process transitions and invalid states;
- assurance claims and the evidence that supports them; and
- federation recognition, conflict, dissent, exit, and migration.

Interfaces, credential systems, storage, analytic models, voting protocols, and hosting arrangements may then change without silently changing those civic meanings. A migration that preserves bytes but loses authority, dissent, or provenance is not a successful migration.

### 9.2 Reference environment

The research environment should include:

- documented synthetic communities and adversarial scenarios;
- deterministic test fixtures for process and authority states;
- accessible web and non-web participant paths;
- multiple identity, record, and voting experiments behind common contracts;
- observable failure injection, recovery, and audit tooling;
- privacy-preserving test data with explicit re-identification limits;
- an export/import harness; and
- a public evidence site that links claims to versions, tests, incidents, and open objections.

It should be possible to reproduce a result without trusting the original developer's machine or unwritten knowledge.

### 9.3 Reuse before reinvention

Existing standards and components should be reused when they satisfy a defined requirement and their license, maintenance, security, accessibility, deployment, and governance are acceptable. “Open source” is not enough. A reusable component also needs comprehensible scope, active stewardship or a credible fork plan, tests, update practices, integration boundaries, and a known failure response.

When no component fits, AUU should document why before building a replacement. Experimental components should remain isolated behind narrow interfaces until their assumptions have been tested.

### 9.4 Software supply chain and reproducibility

Each releasable build needs versioned source, reproducible or independently verifiable build instructions, dependency inventory, signed release information, vulnerability handling, update and rollback procedures, and a supported-lifetime statement. Deployment owners must know what happens when a dependency is abandoned, compromised, relicensed, or no longer legal to operate.

## 10. Evidence and research method

The companion [research index](5.research.md) should track the current evidence base. This strategy defines how that evidence is used.

### 10.1 Evidence classes

| Class | Useful for | Common limit |
|---|---|---|
| Standards and formal specifications | Shared definitions, protocol requirements, conformance targets. | Publication does not prove a particular implementation or deployment. |
| Peer-reviewed and preprint research | Methods, results, theory, and open disputes. | Setting, sample, implementation, and replication may limit transfer. |
| Independent audits and evaluations | Concrete evidence about an artifact or deployment. | Evidence usually applies only to the reviewed version and scope. |
| Field studies and case records | Human behavior, institutions, adoption, and unintended effects. | Causal attribution and generalization may be difficult. |
| Maintainer documentation | Current product scope, interfaces, license, and operational guidance. | It is a primary claim, not independent proof of effectiveness. |
| AUU experiments | Evidence about AUU's own assumptions and artifacts. | Builder bias, small samples, and artificial settings require explicit treatment. |

Sources should be tagged **current**, **superseded**, **withdrawn**, **unverified**, or **contested**. A citation must support the specific sentence attached to it. A design cannot inherit assurance merely by citing a respected standard or paper.

### 10.2 Reproducibility and independent challenge

Consequential experiments should publish, where lawful and safe:

- the protocol and preregistered questions;
- artifact versions and configuration;
- test data or a safe generator;
- analysis code and decision rules;
- deviations and missing data;
- negative and ambiguous results;
- conflicts of interest and funding; and
- enough instructions for an independent team to repeat or challenge the work.

Protected evidence may require controlled review rather than public release. The public record should still identify what was withheld, why, who reviewed it, what conclusion depended on it, and how that conclusion can be challenged without exposing the protected material.

### 10.3 Research with people

Not every usability session is formal human-subject research, but the ethical questions do not disappear when a study has another label. Before collecting data from people, the team should determine applicable legal and institutional review, minimize collection, obtain meaningful consent, protect withdrawal, plan compensation, control secondary use, and define who responds if participation creates harm.

No experimental feature should be presented as settled civic infrastructure merely to obtain realistic behavior. Deception, if ever justified in a narrowly reviewed study, must not concern a participant's legal rights, safety, real authority, or actual data exposure.

## 11. Program sequence

This is an order of dependence, not a promise of dates. Team size, evidence, funding, partner readiness, and discovered risk will change the pace.

### Phase A: establish the research constitution

Complete W0 governance, the rights-floor process, claims register, research ethics procedure, funding/capture policy, decision records, and public challenge route. Publish the shared civic vocabulary and open disagreements.

**Exit:** AUU can explain who currently makes research decisions, how those decisions are bounded, and how they can be challenged.

### Phase B: prove the model can be understood

Build paper and low-fidelity versions of the complete civic cycle. Test the eight reader questions, visibility model, authority distinctions, challenge lifecycle, and accessible alternatives with varied participants.

**Exit:** Most participants in the intended test population can reconstruct consequential scope, visibility, authority, and remedy without expert coaching; serious misconceptions are understood and repaired.

### Phase C: build the non-binding civic loop

Integrate bounded identity, issue origination, deliberation, source-linked synthesis, an advisory decision, delegated follow-through, records, public challenge, and repair. Use synthetic and then low-risk live settings. Do not make binding-election claims.

**Exit:** The complete loop can fail visibly, recover, preserve dissent, and produce an evidence package a skeptical outsider can inspect.

### Phase D: add collectives, conflicting authority, and operations

Test multiple collective profiles, affected-nonparticipant standing, public claims, role changes, delegation, competing recognition, legal-office imports, continuity, funding, incident response, migration, and retirement.

**Exit:** A real but non-binding pilot can state exactly whose process it is, what authority it lacks, who may object, who operates it, and what happens when actors or infrastructure fail.

### Phase E: investigate high-assurance decisions separately

Run voting and eligibility laboratories with explicit threat models, independent expertise, malicious clients, coercion scenarios, accessibility tests, recovery, and receipt tradeoffs. Keep preference polling and low-risk decisions separate from public-office election claims.

**Exit:** Any proposed use has a bounded assurance case and independent review. An unresolved conflict between participant verifiability and coercion resistance remains labelled unresolved.

### Phase F: test federation without absorption

Connect independently governed collectives only after each can represent its own authority, dissent, challenge, and exit. Test competing claims and failed recognition, not only friendly data exchange.

**Exit:** Federation can increase coordination without turning technical compatibility into authority or forcing a collective to surrender its own records and recourse.

## 12. Program health and accountability

### 12.1 Public program dashboard

The program should publish a compact dashboard showing:

- workstream owner and decision authority;
- current maturity by dimension;
- active claims and attached evidence;
- blocked dependencies;
- open high-severity risks and objections;
- upcoming gate decisions;
- incidents, pauses, and retired claims; and
- funding and material conflicts.

Progress reporting should count resolved uncertainty and invalidated assumptions, not only code, pilots, users, or money raised.

### 12.2 Periodic model review

At each major gate, a cross-disciplinary review should ask:

1. Does the current system still implement the ethical commitments?
2. Can a least-prepared intended participant understand the consequence?
3. Have we confused participation with consent, popularity with authority, a record with truth, or interoperability with recognition?
4. Which assurance claim is now weaker than our language suggests?
5. What new concentration of power has the research process itself created?
6. What should be simplified, split, paused, or removed?

The answers, objections, and resulting decisions belong in the public record, subject to the defined visibility rules.

### 12.3 Contribution entry points

People should be able to contribute before a software workstream is ready. Useful contributions include:

- finding, classifying, and challenging evidence;
- documenting a lived failure, institutional process, or affected-person need;
- reviewing language, translations, accessibility, and comprehension;
- writing scenarios, requirements, state models, threat cases, and test fixtures;
- testing prototypes and attempting to misuse them under an approved protocol;
- comparing existing tools and running adoption rehearsals;
- reviewing governance, law, funding, operations, maintenance, and exit; and
- implementing an artifact only after its scope, owner, evidence need, and gate are clear.

Each contribution should attach to a workstream question or artifact and show its status. The program should not reward code volume while treating criticism, facilitation, care, translation, documentation, or negative results as peripheral work.

## 13. What this strategy deliberately refuses to promise

This strategy does not promise a single technology stack, a universal identity system, anonymous voting with every desirable property, unbiased automated synthesis, automatic institutional compliance, or a timeline for global adoption. It does not treat publication, decentralization, cryptography, open source, or participation as automatic legitimacy.

It promises a more disciplined path: define the civic claim, expose its authority and risks, test it with the people who must understand it, invite independent challenge, increase consequence only when evidence increases, and retain a path to repair or stop.

AUU should be judged not by the grandeur of its proposed feature list, but by whether each tested part helps affected people originate, deliberate, authorize, delegate, act, record, challenge, repair, and federate without surrendering their agency or mistaking software for a ruler.
